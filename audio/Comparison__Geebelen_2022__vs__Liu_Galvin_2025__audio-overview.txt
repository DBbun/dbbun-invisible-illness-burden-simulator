This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Belgian Lyme Borreliosis Cost-of-Illness Explorer, on the Lyme disease side, and second, twenty twenty-two Long COVID Lost-Labor Cost Simulator, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers only - one Lyme disease paper reporting direct medical, non-medical, and productivity costs in twenty nineteen euros, and one Long COVID paper reporting lost-labor or productivity costs in twenty twenty-two United States dollars. Both papers present pooled national or population-level cost figures without demographic subgroup breakdowns, and both explicitly state that parts of total economic burden (such as long-term, caregiving, or direct medical costs) were left unmeasured. The two papers examine different cost categories, currencies, price years, and time windows, so their dollar figures cannot be added, divided, or otherwise combined into a single cross-condition number. Each paper also flags a different systemic gap specific to its own condition - a diagnostic-coding gap for Lyme disease and a lack of effective treatment for Long COVID.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has eight dimensions; these are the first six. On out-of-pocket cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper reports that twenty-four point five percent of direct medical cost is not reimbursed by insurance, with patients paying forty-three point nine euros (twenty nineteen) out of pocket for early-stage (erythema migrans) Lyme disease and two hundred eighty-six euros (twenty nineteen) for disseminated or late Lyme disease, plus one hundred ninety euros (twenty nineteen) in travel costs for disseminated or late patients. The Long COVID paper does not report any out-of-pocket or direct medical cost figures; it states that direct medical and non-medical costs were not quantified in the document itself. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On work and income loss. The Lyme disease paper reports that productivity losses make up forty-six point four percent of total Lyme borreliosis cost (twenty nineteen euros) but does not give a per-person lost-labor dollar figure comparable to the Long COVID paper's method. The Long COVID paper reports a mean lost-labor cost gap of one thousand two hundred forty-two dollars per person or year (twenty twenty-two) for Long COVID versus never having had COVID nineteen, and a national twenty twenty-two lost-labor cost of twelve billion seven hundred eighty-four million one hundred sixty-eight thousand six hundred seventy-five dollars attributable to Long COVID. The Lyme disease paper reports that per-patient cost for disseminated or late Lyme disease (five thousand one hundred forty-eight euros, twenty nineteen) is about twenty-six point seven times the per-patient cost for early-stage erythema migrans (one hundred ninety-three euros, twenty nineteen), linking later-stage diagnosis to markedly higher per-patient cost. The Long COVID paper does not report a diagnostic-delay or disease-stage cost comparison; it only distinguishes acute COVID nineteen without Long COVID from Long COVID (symptoms lasting three or more months) using self-report. The Long COVID paper reports eighty-one point two percent paid sick leave prevalence overall versus seventy-nine point six percent among those with Long COVID (a one point six percentage point gap) and notes the paid-sick-leave or Long COVID association did not reach statistical significance (odds ratio zero point nine). The Lyme disease paper states there is no specific International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code for early-stage (erythema migrans) or post-treatment Lyme disease, so all hospital costs were allocated to the broader disseminated or late Lyme disease category and coding quality could not be checked, meaning claims-based hospital cost figures produce lower or different counts in the setting studied. The Long COVID paper relies on self-reported COVID nineteen or Long COVID history and self-reported missed workdays rather than official claims data, and flags this reliance on self-report as a limitation rather than an official-data undercount. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On what the evidence leaves unmeasured. The Lyme disease paper states it did not collect cost data beyond six to twelve months post-diagnosis, excluded pediatric costs, and based post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome cost estimates on very small samples (six and three patients). The Long COVID paper states it has no data on direct medical costs, direct non-medical costs, caregiving time, diagnostic delay, or multi-year projections, offering only a single twenty twenty-two calendar-year snapshot of missed-workday costs. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers.

What the two papers share: Both papers present their headline cost figures as single pooled national totals without any breakdown by sex, age, race or ethnicity, or disability status. Both papers explicitly state that important cost components are missing from their burden estimate.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper provides a breakdown of economic burden by sex, age, race or ethnicity, or disability status. Neither paper provides a multi-year or lifetime cost projection; the Lyme disease paper is limited to a six to twelve month follow-up window and the Long COVID paper to a single twenty twenty-two calendar year.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, two of them here. Lyme disease paper flags a fifth of medical costs unreimbursed. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers add a measure of out-of-pocket or non-reimbursed direct medical costs to see whether a similar share goes unpaid by insurance? Long COVID paper measures a lost-labor cost gap by infection status. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could Lyme disease researchers estimate a comparable lost-labor cost gap between people with and without Lyme disease?

The limits: This comparison covers two papers only - one Lyme disease paper (one paper total, tagged for direct_costs, with all three limitations flagged as applying) and one Long COVID paper (one paper total, tagged for employment, with two of the three limitations flagged as applying) - each built from a single uploaded document. This is a small, non-random sample reflecting whichever documents happened to be uploaded, not a controlled or representative comparison of either condition, and no paper in this sample covers both conditions. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries.
