This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Persistent Lyme Disease Care-Access Barriers Simulator, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID Care-Coordination and Symptom-Duration Explorer, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers - one Lyme disease paper built from a twenty twenty-one self-selected clinician survey, and one Long COVID paper built from a qualitative interview study at a single academic clinic. The Lyme disease paper offers quantified clinician-reported cost and access-barrier figures, while the Long COVID paper offers only qualitative care-coordination and diagnostic-uncertainty findings with no cost data. Both papers separately describe a systemic gap in provider knowledge or guidelines for the condition studied, and both flag that their samples are small, non-random, and not broken down by subgroup. Because only one paper exists per condition here, this cannot show whether these patterns hold more broadly for either Lyme disease or Long COVID.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers: one Lyme disease paper built from a twenty twenty-one clinician survey, and one Long COVID paper built from a qualitative patient-interview study, so no general conclusions about either condition can be drawn from this small set. The Lyme disease paper reports quantified out-of-pocket cost figures (a fifteen thousand dollars dollars midpoint annual estimate and a forty-five thousand dollars dollars projected multi-year estimate), while the Long COVID paper reports no cost figures at all. Both papers separately describe a systemic gap in provider knowledge, training, or treatment guidelines for the condition studied, and both flag that their findings are not broken down by patient or clinician subgroup.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has eight dimensions; these are the first six. On out-of-pocket cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper reports a clinician-quoted annual out-of-pocket cost midpoint of fifteen thousand dollars dollars (price year not stated) and a projected cumulative multi-year out-of-pocket cost of forty-five thousand dollars dollars, drawn from a single clinician's anecdotal open-text estimate rather than a population-representative figure. The Long COVID paper reports no direct medical, non-medical, or out-of-pocket cost figures for any patient; its own evidence gaps state that no cost data exist in the source document. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On diagnostic delay or symptom duration before care. The Lyme disease paper cites a prior patient survey finding that seventy-eight percent of patients were not diagnosed within the first six months of illness, and a registry figure that seventy-two percent were misdiagnosed with another condition first; these are cited from other sources, not this paper's own data. The Long COVID paper reports that many interviewed patients had experienced severe symptoms for one to two years, but does not quantify time from symptom onset to diagnosis beyond its own three-month clinic eligibility threshold. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On access barriers. The Lyme disease paper's own clinician survey found seventy-four percent of clinicians do not participate in insurance networks and thirty-nine percent had been reported to a medical board, insurer, or quality-improvement committee. The Long COVID paper describes access barriers qualitatively, including patients feeling that providers did not believe or validate their symptoms, but does not quantify any access-barrier rate. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On care coordination and complexity of care. The Lyme disease paper's clinician survey found seventy-nine percent of clinicians cited complexity of care as a challenge, including patient cognitive impairment and frequent between-visit calls, though this paper is not tagged with a care-fragmentation category. The Long COVID paper is tagged with the care_fragmentation category and describes patients' confusion about which symptoms to report to which specialist across referrals to cardiology, physical therapy, rehabilitation psychology, and speech therapy. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On work and income loss. The Lyme disease paper's own evidence gaps state there is no quantification of indirect costs such as lost income, productivity loss, or caregiver time for patients. The Long COVID paper's own evidence gaps state that no indirect cost figures, including lost income, productivity loss, or caregiving time, are reported. The comparison marks this as unclear from these two papers. On subgroup breakdown of findings. The Lyme disease paper reports all clinician-survey percentages as pooled national figures with no breakdown by clinician or patient sex, age, race or ethnicity, or disability status. The Long COVID paper reports its one to two year symptom-duration observation as a pooled characteristic of the twenty-one-patient sample with no breakdown by sex, age, race or ethnicity, or disability status, even though sex and age are reported for the sample as a whole. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers.

What the two papers share: Each paper describes a systemic gap in provider knowledge, training, or treatment guidelines for the condition it studies. Each paper draws on a small, non-random sample and states this limits how far its findings can be generalized.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper reports quantified income loss, productivity loss, or caregiver time burden. Neither paper provides a subgroup breakdown (by sex, age, race or ethnicity, or disability status) of its reported figures.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, three of them here. Ask whether specialists are inside or outside insurance networks. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could the Long COVID clinic ask how many of the specialists patients see actually accept their insurance? Regulatory scrutiny of treating clinicians can be counted directly. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers ask treating clinicians whether they have faced similar board, insurer, or committee inquiries? Clinician-quoted annual and multi-year costs can be put in dollars. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could the Long COVID clinic collect clinician- or patient-quoted annual and multi-year out-of-pocket cost estimates the way the Lyme disease paper did?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an artificial intelligence model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used three model calls. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This is a small, non-random set of uploaded documents, not a controlled study: the Lyme disease group contains one paper and the Long COVID group contains one paper, with zero cross-condition papers and zero other papers, so every comparison above rests on a single source per condition and cannot be generalized beyond the two documents reviewed. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision.
