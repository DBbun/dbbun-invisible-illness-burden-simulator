This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Lyme Disease Economic Burden Simulator (Maryland Eastern Shore, nineteen ninety-seven to two thousand).

This is a two thousand six Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-authored peer-reviewed study that measured the economic burden of Lyme disease among patients in five Maryland Eastern Shore counties from nineteen ninety-seven to two thousand, using medical-record charge data plus a patient-mailed cost questionnaire (reproduced as Appendix one).

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulator models a two thousand six Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-authored peer-reviewed cost-of-illness study of Lyme disease among patients in five Maryland Eastern Shore counties, nineteen ninety-seven to two thousand, covering per-patient direct medical, indirect medical (extra drug costs), nonmedical (paid help, travel), and productivity-loss costs by disease stage, plus the study's own extrapolation to a nationwide total using a two thousand two national case count.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: This historical study of Lyme disease patients in five Maryland counties (nineteen ninety-seven to two thousand) found that the typical ('median') total cost - combining medical bills, extra out-of-pocket drug costs, paid help or travel, and lost work time - was higher for patients diagnosed with late-stage Lyme disease than for those diagnosed at an early stage, with late-stage typical cost running a little more than twice the early-stage typical cost in this cohort.

For care delivery teams: In this nineteen ninety-seven to two thousand Maryland cohort, median and mean total costs were substantially higher for late-stage than early-stage clinically diagnosed Lyme disease patients, and mean therapy and hospitalization or emergency room cost components both declined between nineteen ninety-seven and two thousand.

For payers and policy audiences: The study's own nationwide extrapolation, combining a per-patient cost figure from clinically defined early or late-stage Maryland patients with the twenty-three thousand seven hundred sixty-three Lyme disease cases reported to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention nationally in two thousand two, produced a stated headline estimate of about two hundred three million dollars.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Costlier case mix (more late-stage): Per-patient direct medical cost from two thousand nine hundred seventy dollars to four thousand dollars and Per-patient indirect, nonmedical, and productivity-loss cost from five thousand two hundred two dollars to seven thousand dollars. Then Combined per-patient cost used in nationwide extrapolation moves from eight thousand one hundred seventy-two dollars to eleven thousand dollars; and Recomputed nationwide economic impact moves from one hundred ninety-four million dollars to two hundred sixty-one million dollars. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, five hundred fifty-three units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from four from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance, two from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tick surveillance, two from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data, one from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey emergency department data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Then generative artificial intelligence, Claude Sonnet, is called three times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code. Computer code, not the artificial intelligence, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Seven quantities from the document were read for checking; seven were compared with federal data and zero could not be compared. Expected mean total cost per Lyme disease patient, aggregated across all diagnosis groups: one thousand nine hundred sixty-five in two thousand dollars is about three thousand six hundred seventy-four in twenty twenty-five dollars (the consumer price index all items, xone point nine). The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Late-stage minus early-stage median total cost is five hundred twenty-six dollars; Late-stage to early-stage median cost ratio is two point three; Combined per-patient cost used in nationwide extrapolation is eight thousand one hundred seventy-two dollars; Recomputed nationwide economic impact is one hundred ninety-four million dollars.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Costlier case mix (more late-stage) sets Per-patient direct medical cost to four thousand dollars and Per-patient indirect, nonmedical, and productivity-loss cost to seven thousand dollars, so Combined per-patient cost used in nationwide extrapolation becomes eleven thousand dollars; Recomputed nationwide economic impact becomes two hundred sixty-one million dollars. Lower-cost case mix (more early-stage) sets Per-patient direct medical cost to two thousand dollars and Per-patient indirect, nonmedical, and productivity-loss cost to three thousand five hundred dollars, so Combined per-patient cost used in nationwide extrapolation becomes five thousand five hundred dollars; Recomputed nationwide economic impact becomes one hundred thirty-one million dollars. Higher national case count (believed undercount) sets National Lyme disease cases reported to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to thirty-five thousand cases, so Recomputed nationwide economic impact becomes two hundred eighty-six million dollars. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Late-stage minus early-stage median total cost, those draws run from one hundred twenty-three dollars to nine hundred sixty-one dollars, with a mean of five hundred fifty-two dollars. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: direct costs of care. The other fifteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an artificial intelligence model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
