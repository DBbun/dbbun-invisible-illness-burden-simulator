This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Chronic Lyme Disease Symptom Burden: Patient-Reported Utilization, Cost, and Employment Impact (a cited study twenty fourteen, PeerJ).

This is a twenty fourteen PeerJ study by a cited study that surveyed three thousand ninety self-selected online respondents clinically diagnosed with Lyme disease whose symptoms persisted more than six months after antibiotic treatment ('chronic Lyme disease,' chronic Lyme disease).

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulator reproduces patient-reported survey percentages from a twenty fourteen self-selected internet survey of three thousand ninety people clinically diagnosed with Lyme disease whose symptoms persisted more than six months after antibiotic treatment ('chronic Lyme disease' or post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome), compared against general-population reference figures the authors cite from separate federal and published sources.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: In this self-selected online survey of people with persisting Lyme symptoms more than six months after treatment, a majority reported high out-of-pocket spending, frequent doctor visits, and reduced ability to work compared with general-population reference figures the authors cite.

For care delivery teams: This patient-reported survey found markedly higher outpatient visit frequency, out-of-pocket spending above common thresholds, and work disruption among people with persisting post-treatment Lyme symptoms, compared with general-population reference data the authors cite.

For payers and policy audiences: This self-selected patient survey reports substantially higher healthcare utilization, out-of-pocket spending above two thousand dollarsand five thousand dollarsper year, and lower employment among people with persisting post-treatment Lyme symptoms, compared with general-population reference figures the authors cite from federal sources.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Entire adult sample employment: chronic Lyme disease employment rate, ages twenty-five-fifty-four from forty-five point nine percent to forty-two point one percent. Then General population minus chronic Lyme disease employment rate, ages twenty-five-fifty-four moves from thirty-five point one percentage points to thirty-eight point nine percentage points. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, three hundred eighty-four units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from four from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance, two from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tick surveillance, two from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, two from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about seventy-one cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Four quantities from the document were read for checking; two were compared with federal data and two could not be compared. Out-of-pocket Lyme-related expense threshold: two thousand dollars per year: two thousand in twenty thirteen dollars is about two thousand seven hundred sixty-four in twenty twenty-five dollars (the consumer price index all items, xone point four). Centers for Disease Control and Prevention pre-twenty thirteen-revision annual Lyme disease surveillance count: not computed. The document gives no case count with a year or a year range. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, chronic Lyme disease minus general-population share exceeding two thousand dollars per year out-of-pocket is forty-nine percentage points; chronic Lyme disease-to-general-population annual doctor-visit ratio is five point two ratio (chronic Lyme disease visits per general-population visit); General population minus chronic Lyme disease employment rate, ages twenty-five-fifty-four is thirty-five point one percentage points; Share of sample not diagnosed within two years of symptom onset is sixty-one point seven percent.

There are one named scenarios, one click each. Entire adult sample employment sets chronic Lyme disease employment rate, ages twenty-five-fifty-four to forty-two point one percent, so General population minus chronic Lyme disease employment rate, ages twenty-five-fifty-four becomes thirty-eight point nine percentage points. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

The simulator has two forecast charts: Cumulative doctor visits per chronic Lyme disease patient, assuming the reported annual rate repeats unchanged each year; Cumulative doctor visits per general-population patient, assuming the reported annual rate repeats unchanged each year. At the default assumptions it goes from zero visits per patient to ninety-seven visits per patient over five years. A forecast here is a straight-line continuation of the document's stated step, not a prediction. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For chronic Lyme disease minus general-population share exceeding two thousand dollars per year out-of-pocket, those draws run from seven point three percentage points to eighty-three point seven percentage points, with a mean of forty-five point nine percentage points. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for three of them: diagnostic delay, direct costs of care, work and income. The other thirteen are empty here. They include caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
