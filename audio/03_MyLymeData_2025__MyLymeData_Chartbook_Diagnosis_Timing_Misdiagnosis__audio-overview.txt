This is an educational what-if model, not medical advice.

This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator built from the My Lyme Data twenty twenty-five research chartbook. That is a report from a patient registry of more than nineteen thousand self-enrolled people with Lyme disease. The chartbook describes itself as advocacy material from a patient organization, not an independent economic study, and most of its numbers are percentages that patients reported in surveys.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates, the value is turning registry patterns into questions to bring to a clinician. The document's own prompts are to ask how your own diagnosis timeline might relate to these patterns, and to ask whether a clinician accepts your insurance. These are patterns reported by registry participants. They are not a guarantee for any individual.

For care delivery teams, the value is a way to compare the registry's gaps with their own referral and diagnostic timelines, and to check whether intake for suspected persistent Lyme disease includes co-infection screening and care coordination for patients with many visits.

For payers and policy audiences, the value is the insurance finding, which can inform network-adequacy reviews, and a reminder to say that the one billion dollar cost estimate is claims-based and likely undercounts.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? Compare it with the two things you could do instead.

You could read the paper. You would see that seventy-seven percent of patients diagnosed late reported persistent Lyme disease, against roughly twenty percent of those diagnosed early. But the numbers are frozen. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. Every observed value is an input on the screen, with its allowed range. Set the early rate to thirty percent and the late rate to fifty, and the gap falls from fifty-seven points to twenty. Raise the general-population comparator from one point oh nine visits a year to three, and the ratio of visits falls from about seventeen point eight to about six point five. Nothing is re-typed or re-calculated by hand, and every result updates at once.

Or you could review the federal resources yourself. The simulator's library holds about fourteen hundred and fifty-four of them, and for this chartbook, four hundred twenty-three were relevant. Few people would read those. The simulator picks the ten most relevant, shows which datasets they came from, and tells you for each whether the analysis actually relied on it. It runs the comparisons that are possible by computer code, and it surfaces the limits of a source, such as a survey that is too small to estimate Lyme costs.

Now, how it works, and where machine learning and generative artificial intelligence come in.

You upload a document, and its text is extracted on the local machine. A small local classifier decides whether it is about Lyme disease, Long COVID, or both. It uses keyword matching and a text-similarity method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That method scores how important a word is to one document compared with all the others. This is the machine learning step. It is a classical text-matching method, not a large language model, and it makes no model call.

That decision determines which federal evidence is eligible. The simulator draws on a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units. A unit is one file from a federal dataset, such as a data table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, or one file inside a compressed archive, that has been pre-processed into a short text summary. The units come from twelve federal agencies, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the National Center for Health Statistics, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, the United States Census Bureau, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Bureau of Economic Analysis, the Health Resources and Services Administration, the Social Security Administration, and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

For this chartbook, four hundred twenty-three units were eligible. A retrieval step scored them and picked ten as short excerpts, from four datasets: six from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance, two from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tick surveillance, one from the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, and one from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data. Only the short summaries are read, never the raw federal files, and nothing searches the live internet while a simulator is generated.

Only then does generative artificial intelligence come in. Claude Sonnet is called four times. First, it extracts the document's findings. Second, it drafts the interactive simulator and its documentation. Third, it writes named what-if scenarios and forecasts. Fourth, separately, it writes a Python version of the model that you can download. The document's extracted text, tables and figures go to the model. The original file is not uploaded. A run takes roughly four to nine minutes.

Then the arithmetic is done by code, not by the artificial intelligence. Dollar figures are restated in current dollars using the consumer price index. Reported Lyme case counts are compared with counts reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. If something cannot be checked, it is listed as not computed, with the reason. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the chartbook. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. For this chartbook, the one check, the document's cited estimate from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention of five hundred thousand Lyme cases a year, could not be computed, because the document gives no case count with a year, and the simulator says so. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Federal data also explains why some things cannot be estimated. The Medical Expenditure Panel Survey, a national health spending survey run by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, only releases a three-character diagnosis code when at least thirty sampled people carry it. For the code covering other spirochetal infections, which includes Lyme disease, there were thirty-one sampled people in each of twenty sixteen, twenty seventeen and twenty eighteen, and none in twenty nineteen through twenty twenty-one. That is too few for good Lyme cost estimates, so the simulator treats that survey as context, not a source of numbers.

The federal library can grow. In the full tool, an Administrator page lets Dr. Kartoun register a completely new federal resource, or re-process an existing dataset after its files are updated. This step is plain file parsing, and no artificial intelligence model is called. So the evidence base can be extended with new resources, or deepened with more of the ones already there.

Now the what-if scenarios.

The simulator opens at the chartbook's reported values. Among patients diagnosed late, six months or more after illness began, seventy-seven percent later reported persistent Lyme disease, compared with roughly twenty percent of those diagnosed early. That is a gap of fifty-seven percentage points, within a self-selected registry. The document describes it as an association and does not test cause. Misdiagnosis is seventy-four percent for persistent Lyme patients against forty-two percent for acute patients, a gap of thirty-two points. Patients with persistent Lyme disease report nineteen point four doctor visits a year against one point oh nine for the general population, about seventeen point eight times as many. Half of persistent Lyme patients in the registry say their physician does not accept insurance. And there is one dollar figure, about one billion dollars a year, cited from a separate claims-based study that the document says likely undercounts.

There are six named scenarios, one click each. The widest diagnosis-timing gap sets the early rate to five percent and the late rate to ninety percent, which makes the gap eighty-five points. The narrowest sets them to thirty and fifty percent, a gap of twenty points. The wider misdiagnosis gap sets ninety percent against twenty percent, which makes seventy points. More physicians declining insurance raises the share from fifty to seventy percent. Fewer declining lowers it to twenty percent. And the lower claims-based cost scenario drops the one billion dollar estimate to five hundred million dollars, consistent with the document's caution that claims data miss patients whose providers do not accept insurance. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

You can also move any single input yourself from the user interface and watch which results change. A sensitivity view moves one input at a time across its range while the others stay put, and ranks inputs by effect. It is an exploration of the model's own assumptions, not a measure of real-world uncertainty.

There is also a forecast, and yes, it uses Monte Carlo simulation. At nineteen point four visits a year, held flat, a persistent Lyme patient's cumulative visits reach ninety-seven after five years. That is a straight-line continuation, not a prediction. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range, and the shaded band shows the middle eighty percent of the results. The visit rate is allowed to range from five to thirty a year, which would mean five-year totals anywhere from twenty-five to one hundred fifty, and the band covers the middle part of that spread. The band is not a probability or a confidence interval. The five hundred draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

There is also a cross-pollination analysis. It sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. In the full tool you tick one of each and choose the cross-pollination analysis. On the public demo there are thirteen saved comparisons to read, and this chartbook appears in two of them. A comparison shows what each paper says about the same questions: cost, work, diagnostic delay, access to care, and how official counts undercount. It shows which of the sixteen burden types each paper measures, with the key figures copied from each simulator, and it lists questions worth asking. For example, set beside a Long COVID study that identifies patients from health records instead of diagnosis codes, this chartbook reports a thirty-two point misdiagnosis gap and a fifty-seven point gap by diagnosis timing, while the Long COVID study reports no diagnostic-delay figure. So the comparison marks that as one-sided and asks whether Long COVID researchers could measure time to diagnosis the way the Lyme chartbook did. It also notes that official counts fall short in both papers, but for different reasons: insurance claims that miss unbilled care in one, and insensitive diagnosis codes in the other. The numbers are copied from the simulators. The wording is written by an artificial intelligence model and checked in code, and any number it cannot match is flagged. The papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

What can you take with you, and how does it connect to other systems?

The download bundle includes a formatted document, a nine-sheet Excel workbook with the parameters, data, results, evidence and limits, comma-separated files, the model's specification, and Python code that re-runs the same arithmetic. Because the results are in Excel and comma-separated files, they can be opened by statistical software and by languages such as R or Python, for further analysis. Every download records which model calls produced it.

There is also a small Application Programming Interface in the full tool, which runs on Dr. Kartoun's computer. Another system or script can send its own assumptions to a finished simulator and get every output and forecast back at once, using the same arithmetic as the screen and rejecting values outside an input's allowed range. It can also submit papers and collect the finished simulators later. Every call needs a key made on Dr. Kartoun's own machine.

For health-data standards, the simulator exports each model's inputs and default outputs as a bundle in the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources standard, version R4, with the source paper and provenance. It passes the official validator for that version with no errors. It contains no patient, is not clinical data, and is a demonstration, not a certified implementation, and the simulator does not claim alignment with a standard it does not implement.

It also demonstrates Blue Button 2.0. Blue Button 2.0 is the interface of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services that lets a Medicare beneficiary share their own claims with an app, with their consent. In the demonstration, a person signs in through a standard authorization flow against the federal sandbox, which holds only synthetic test beneficiaries. The simulator reads their coverage and claims in that same standard, reduces them to yearly totals held in memory, and shows them beside the simulator's dollar figures. Nothing is stored, and nothing is sent to an artificial intelligence model. It has no access to real Medicare data and has not been through federal production review. The point is the path: a person's own claims, shared with consent, can sit beside a paper's numbers.

What does the chartbook not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This chartbook has results for three of them: direct costs of care, diagnostic delay, and barriers to reaching care. The other thirteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the chartbook may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an artificial intelligence model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

Finally, the potential. The concept could be transformative: in principle, the same approach can be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, turning a static report into an adjustable model that is checked against federal data and states its own gaps. That is a potential. It has been shown for those two conditions, and two papers on a different condition were run as a labeled demonstration, but no condition-specific federal evidence was curated for them, nobody has validated them, and every result still depends on what the paper itself reports. This simulator is also part of D B bun's infrastructure. The concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries, so that readers could explore the numbers in a paper instead of only reading them. That is a vision for the future.
