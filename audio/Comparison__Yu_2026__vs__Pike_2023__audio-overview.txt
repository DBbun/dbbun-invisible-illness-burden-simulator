This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Lyme Disease Direct Health Care Cost Explorer, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Post-COVID nineteen Excess Direct Medical Cost Explorer, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers: one Lyme disease paper built from a claims-based cohort study, and one Long COVID paper built from a privately insured claims cohort from the first pandemic year. Both papers report substantial direct medical cost increases associated with the condition studied, with costs rising sharply for more severe cases (disseminated Lyme disease or hospitalized COVID nineteen), and both explicitly state they do not measure indirect costs like lost income or caregiving. They differ in what kind of undercounting affects their official-data figures: the Lyme disease paper relies on an unvalidated claims-based case-classification algorithm, while the Long COVID paper had no specific diagnostic code available during its study period and used a proxy method the authors call a lower bound.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has eight dimensions; these are the first six. On out-of-pocket cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper reports a patient out-of-pocket cost of one hundred eighty-eight dollars per episode overall (twenty twenty-two dollars), rising to four hundred one dollars per episode for disseminated disease, but notes this figure is only available for the fifteen point five percent of case patients with out-of-pocket claims data. The Long COVID paper does not isolate out-of-pocket costs; its direct medical cost figures already combine payer- and patient-paid amounts, and the paper explicitly treats out-of-pocket burden as a separate, unestimated category. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On severity or hospitalization cost gap. The Lyme disease paper reports a disseminated-minus-localized direct cost difference of six thousand one hundred thirty-eight dollars per episode and a disseminated-to-localized cost ratio of nine point eight (twenty twenty-two dollars for the dollar figure). The Long COVID paper reports a hospitalization cost multiplier of three point two for adults and nine point five for children at six months (twenty twenty dollars underlying the associated absolute differences). The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On work and income or productivity loss. The Lyme disease paper states explicitly that its cost estimation 'does not include all states or indirect costs,' meaning lost income and caregiving time are not measured anywhere in the paper. The Long COVID paper explicitly lists productivity losses for patients or caregivers as not captured by its data. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On how official data undercounts the condition. The Lyme disease paper identifies cases using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision diagnosis codes and an antibiotic-prescription algorithm that the authors themselves say 'may not be generalizable to all patients with Lyme disease,' and the localized or disseminated classification algorithm 'has not been validated.' The Long COVID paper notes that no post-COVID nineteen-condition-specific International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code existed during its study period, so cases were inferred by comparing costs of diagnosed COVID nineteen patients to matched controls, which the authors describe as producing a lower bound in the setting studied. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On diagnostic delay evidence. The Lyme disease paper's methods include the thirty days before diagnosis in the follow-up window to capture possible diagnostic-workup costs, implying a diagnostic-delay-related cost, but this is not separately quantified as its own figure. The Long COVID paper summary provided does not describe any diagnostic-delay cost measurement. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On extrapolated or aggregate economic burden scope. The Lyme disease paper extrapolates to an annual cost of five hundred ninety-one million dollars-one billion fifty million dollars across sixteen high-incidence states and the District of Columbia (twenty twenty-two dollars), explicitly excluding other states and indirect costs. The Long COVID paper reports an aggregate cohort total of up to forty-seven million dollars for children and up to three hundred eight million dollars for adults (twenty twenty dollars), but states this applies only to its specific matched study cohort and is not a national or annual population-wide estimate. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers.

What the two papers share: Both papers explicitly say they do not measure indirect costs such as lost income, caregiving time, or productivity loss. Both papers link a more severe form of the condition (disseminated Lyme disease, or hospitalized COVID nineteen) to a much larger direct medical cost than the milder form.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper measures indirect costs such as lost income, caregiving time, or productivity loss for the condition it covers. Neither paper captures costs beyond its stated six-month measurement window, so longer-term or persistent-symptom costs are unmeasured in both.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, four of them here. Ask whether severity drives Long COVID costs as sharply as Lyme disease? The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers calculate an excess-cost gap and ratio between more severe and less severe cases, the way the Lyme disease paper did between disseminated and localized cases? Lyme disease paper tracked patient out-of-pocket cost; Long COVID paper did not. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could the Long COVID cohort data be used to calculate a comparable patient out-of-pocket cost figure alongside its existing excess cost estimates? Long COVID paper found a sex-based cost gap; Lyme disease paper has none. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could the Lyme disease claims data be reanalyzed to see whether direct costs per episode also differ by patient sex? Long COVID paper split costs by age; Lyme disease paper did not. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could the Lyme disease cohort be stratified by age group to see whether the disseminated-versus-localized cost gap differs between children and adults?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an artificial intelligence model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used three model calls. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This comparison is based on a small, non-random sample: exactly one Lyme disease paper and one Long COVID paper, with no papers covering both conditions. Real statistics confirm these sample sizes (one paper per condition, no cross or other papers), so any pattern described here reflects only these two specific documents, not the conditions in general, and this is not a controlled study. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries.
