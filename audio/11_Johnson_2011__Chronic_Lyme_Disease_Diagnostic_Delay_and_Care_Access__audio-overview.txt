This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Chronic Lyme Disease Diagnostic Delay and Care-Access Barriers (two thousand nine to twenty ten United States Patient Survey).

This twenty eleven peer-reviewed study reports results from a two thousand nine to twenty ten web-based survey of two thousand four hundred twenty-four United States residents with clinically diagnosed, lab-confirmed chronic Lyme disease (symptoms persisting six or more months despite at least twenty-one days of antibiotics).

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulation reproduces percentage-based findings from a twenty eleven peer-reviewed web-based survey of two thousand four hundred twenty-four United States residents with clinically diagnosed, lab-confirmed chronic Lyme disease (symptoms persisting six or more months despite at least twenty-one days of antibiotics), surveyed June two thousand nine-March twenty ten.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: In this self-selected online survey of people already diagnosed with chronic Lyme disease, the study reported that many respondents experienced long diagnostic delays, substantial travel for treatment, difficulty getting hospital care, and frequent denial of new insurance coverage or disability benefits linked to one medical society's contested Lyme treatment guidelines.

For care delivery teams: Among this patient-reported sample, a large share described seeing ten or more physicians before diagnosis, difficulty obtaining local hospital treatment, and barriers tied to clinical guideline disputes - including hospitals reportedly restricting privileges for physicians who do not follow one society's guidelines and insurers requiring a specialist consult before covering treatment.

For payers and policy audiences: The study found that, within applicant subgroups, substantial shares of chronic Lyme disease patients reported denial of new insurance coverage and denial of disability benefits specifically for non-conformity with one medical society's treatment guidelines, alongside reports that hospital credentialing and insurer coverage criteria were tied to the same guidelines.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Broader diagnostic-delay threshold (seven or more physicians): Share seeing ten or more physicians before Lyme diagnosis from thirty-seven point five percent to fifty percent. Then Share seeing ten or more physicians before diagnosis moves from thirty-seven point five percent to fifty percent. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, three hundred ninety-seven units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from five from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance, two from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, two from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tick surveillance, one from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about seventy-three cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Five quantities from the document were read for checking; three were compared with federal data and two could not be compared. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-reported new Lyme disease cases, two thousand eight (the United States): the document reports thirty-five thousand one hundred ninety-eight; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's reported cases were thirty-five thousand one hundred ninety-eight (confirmed and probable together), so the document's figure is within ten percent of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention confirmed-plus-probable total (+zero percent versus the total). Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-acknowledged estimated annual Lyme disease cases (low bound, ~sixx reported) (two thousand eight, the United States): the document's estimate of two hundred thousand is five point seven times the thirty-five thousand one hundred ninety-eight cases Centers for Disease Control and Prevention received as reported. Reported cases are a passive-surveillance subset, so estimates from claims or models are usually higher; this shows the size of the gap, not which figure is right. Federal source: public health surveillance (surveillance report), two thousand eight to twenty twenty-one; computed in code from the federal file; no A I model. The model cited two of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Share seeing ten or more physicians before diagnosis is thirty-seven point five percent; Share traveling over fifty miles for treatment is forty-nine point five percent; Share reporting difficulty obtaining hospital treatment is eighty-one point seven percent; Insurance denial percent minus disability-benefit denial percent is one point three percentage points.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Broader diagnostic-delay threshold (seven or more physicians) sets Share seeing ten or more physicians before Lyme diagnosis to fifty percent, so Share seeing ten or more physicians before diagnosis becomes fifty percent. Extreme travel distance (five hundred or more miles) sets Share traveling more than fifty miles for Lyme treatment to nine percent, so Share traveling over fifty miles for treatment becomes nine percent. Frequent emergency room use (six or more visits) sets Share with at least one emergency room visit due to Lyme symptoms to nine point one percent, so Share with at least one emergency room visit tied to Lyme symptoms becomes nine point one percent. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Share seeing ten or more physicians before diagnosis, those draws run from zero percent to ninety-nine point six percent, with a mean of forty-eight point six percent. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for three of them: barriers to reaching care, administrative burden, diagnostic delay. The other thirteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
