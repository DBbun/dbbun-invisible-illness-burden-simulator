This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Long COVID Diagnosis Code (diagnosis code U zero nine point nine) Uptake, Representation, and Care-Pattern Simulator.

This study used a large national electronic health records database (NthreeC, sixteen or more million patients, thirty-four health systems) to examine how the new International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code for long COVID (diagnosis code U zero nine point nine, available starting October twenty twenty-one) was actually used in United States clinical practice between October twenty twenty-one and May twenty twenty-two, in a cohort of thirty-three thousand seven hundred eighty-two coded patients.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulation reflects only diagnostic-coding patterns, demographic or area-level (zip-code) composition, and short-term (sixty-day) care-utilization percentages reported in one electronic health records-based study (NthreeC database, thirty-four United States health systems, October twenty twenty-one-May twenty twenty-two, n=thirty-three thousand seven hundred eighty-two patients coded diagnosis code U zero nine point nine).

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: This study looked at records from people who received the new long COVID diagnosis code (diagnosis code U zero nine point nine) in electronic health records between late twenty twenty-one and mid-twenty twenty-two.

For care delivery teams: Among patients coded diagnosis code U zero nine point nine in this electronic health records cohort, about nine point seven percent had been hospitalized during their earlier acute COVID nineteen infection, and roughly twenty-two point eight percent received an imaging procedure within sixty days of the long COVID diagnosis, giving a rough benchmark for post-diagnosis workup intensity.

For payers and policy audiences: In this thirty-four-site electronic health records cohort, patients coded with the long COVID diagnosis (diagnosis code U zero nine point nine) were significantly more female (sixty-six point nine percent versus fifty-five point six percent comparator, an eleven point three percentage-point gap) and more White (seventy-one point eight percent versus sixty-one point one percent, a ten point seven percentage-point gap) than the overall the COVID nineteen virus-positive population at the same sites, and were under-represented in higher-poverty, higher-unemployment, and higher-public-insurance-reliance zip code areas.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Demographics match overall COVID+ population: Percent female in diagnosis code U zero nine point nine-coded cohort from sixty-six point nine percent to fifty-five point six percent and Percent White in diagnosis code U zero nine point nine-coded cohort from seventy-one point eight percent to sixty-one point one percent. Then diagnosis code U zero nine point nine cohort percent female minus comparator percent female moves from eleven point three percentage points to zero percentage points; and diagnosis code U zero nine point nine cohort percent White minus comparator percent White moves from ten point seven percentage points to zero percentage points. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred fifty-one units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from nine from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey emergency department data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Then generative artificial intelligence, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code. Computer code, not the artificial intelligence, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. No federal check applied to this document. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For diagnosis code U zero nine point nine cohort percent female minus comparator percent female, those draws run from minus eighteen percentage points to thirty-nine percentage points, with a mean of ten point eight percentage points. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: undercounted by official surveillance. The other fifteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an artificial intelligence model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries.
