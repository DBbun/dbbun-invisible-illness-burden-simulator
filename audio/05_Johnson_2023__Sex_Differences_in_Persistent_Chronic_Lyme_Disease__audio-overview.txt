This is an educational what-if model, not medical advice.

This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Sex Differences in Persistent or Chronic Lyme Disease: Diagnostic Delay, Misdiagnosis, and Functional Impairment.

This peer-reviewed study analyzes self-reported survey data from two thousand one hundred seventy United States patients (one thousand eight hundred thirty-nine women, three hundred thirty-one men) in the MyLymeData patient registry who were clinically diagnosed with Lyme disease and remained ill six or more months after antibiotic treatment (persistent or chronic Lyme disease).

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulator models only the sex-disaggregated percentages this peer-reviewed study reports from its own MyLymeData patient registry sample (sample size two thousand one hundred seventy; one thousand eight hundred thirty-nine women, three hundred thirty-one men; dataset dated April twenty-seventh, twenty seventeen), all self-reported by patients already clinically diagnosed with persistent or chronic Lyme disease (illness continuing six or more months after antibiotic treatment).

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: This study reported that among two thousand one hundred seventy people in a self-selected patient registry who were clinically diagnosed with persistent or chronic Lyme disease, women were reported to have higher rates than men of being diagnosed at a late stage, being misdiagnosed beforehand with chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, or a psychiatric condition, and reporting many bed days. One of the document's own next steps: If recovery from Lyme disease has been slow, consider raising with a clinician whether a chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, or psychiatric diagnosis was considered as an alternative explanation rather than alongside a tick-borne illness evaluation - this study reported that pattern was common before a Lyme diagnosis was reached, more so among women.

For care delivery teams: In this registry-based study, women with persistent or chronic Lyme disease were reported at significantly higher rates than men for late-stage diagnosis, prior misdiagnosis (especially with chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, or a psychiatric condition), and high bed-day burden, while rash recollection, lab-test positivity, and antibiotic response or side effects showed no significant sex difference. One of the document's own next steps: Consider reviewing whether intake or differential-diagnosis workflows systematically ask about tick exposure or Lyme-endemic travel before defaulting toward a chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, or psychiatric diagnosis, given this study's finding of high misdiagnosis rates with those conditions before a Lyme diagnosis was reached, more so among women.

For payers and policy audiences: The study found that the female share of patients enrolled in Lyme disease research varied sharply by study design in the sources it reviewed - lower in acute Lyme disease study samples, higher in less-restrictive persistent-illness cohort samples like its own - and that none of the National Institutes of Health-funded trials it reviewed were designed to analyze sex differences. One of the document's own next steps: When reviewing eligibility criteria for a new Lyme disease trial, compare the projected sex composition against this study's documented pattern - lower female enrollment share in the acute Lyme disease study sources it reviewed versus higher female share in the less-restrictive, clinically-oriented persistent-illness study sources it reviewed - noting these come from different study designs and are not interchangeable with each other.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Close the late-stage diagnosis gap: Women diagnosed at a late stage from eighty-three percent to seventy-six percent. Then Late-stage diagnosis gap: women minus men moves from seven percentage points to zero percentage points. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

Now, how it works, and where machine learning and generative A I come in. You upload a document, and its text is extracted on the local machine. A small local classifier decides whether it is about Lyme disease, Long COVID, or both. It uses keyword matching and a text-similarity method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That method scores how important a word is to one document compared with all the others. This is the machine learning step. It is a classical text-matching method, not a large language model, and it makes no model call.

That decision determines which federal evidence is eligible. The simulator draws on a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units. A unit is one file from a federal dataset, such as a data table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, or one file inside a compressed archive, that has been pre-processed into a short text summary. The units come from twelve federal agencies, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the National Center for Health Statistics, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, the United States Census Bureau, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Bureau of Economic Analysis, the Health Resources and Services Administration, the Social Security Administration, and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

For this document, three hundred ninety-seven units were eligible. A retrieval step scored them and picked ten as short excerpts, from these datasets: four from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance, two from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tick surveillance, two from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey emergency department data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, one from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data. Only the short summaries are read, never the raw federal files, and nothing searches the live internet while a simulator is generated.

Only then does generative A I come in. Claude Sonnet is called four times. First, it extracts the document's findings. Second, it drafts the interactive simulator and its documentation. Third, it writes named what-if scenarios and forecasts. Fourth, separately, it writes a Python version of the model that you can download. The document's extracted text, tables and figures go to the model. The original file is not uploaded. For this simulator, the four calls cost about seventy cents in total.

Then the arithmetic is done by code, not by the A I. Three kinds of figures are checked: dollar amounts, which are restated in current dollars using the consumer price index; Lyme case counts, which are compared with counts reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention; and national Long COVID prevalence percentages, which are compared with the Household Pulse Survey. If something cannot be checked, it is listed as not computed, with the reason. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. One quantity from the document was read for checking; zero were compared with federal data and one could not be compared. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimated annual new Lyme disease cases in the United States (cited by this document): not computed. The document gives no case count with a year or a year range. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

The federal library can grow. In the full tool, an Administrator page lets an owner register a completely new federal resource, or re-process an existing dataset after its files are updated. This step is plain file parsing, and no A I model is called. So the evidence base can be extended with new resources, or deepened with more of the ones already there.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Late-stage diagnosis gap: women minus men is seven percentage points; chronic fatigue syndrome or fibromyalgia or psychiatric misdiagnosis gap: women minus men is fifteen percentage points; eight or more bed-day gap: women minus men is fourteen percentage points; Women-enrollment share, persistent-illness studies minus acute-illness studies is thirty-five percentage points.

There are five named scenarios, one click each. Close the late-stage diagnosis gap sets Women diagnosed at a late stage to seventy-six percent, so Late-stage diagnosis gap: women minus men becomes zero percentage points. Close the misdiagnosis gap sets Women misdiagnosed with chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, or psychiatric condition to sixty-three percent, so chronic fatigue syndrome or fibromyalgia or psychiatric misdiagnosis gap: women minus men becomes zero percentage points. Close the bed-days gap sets Women with eight or more bed days in six months to forty-nine percent, so eight or more bed-day gap: women minus men becomes zero percentage points. Lower bound on acute-study enrollment sets Women enrolled in acute Lyme disease study sources to thirty percent, so Women-enrollment share, persistent-illness studies minus acute-illness studies becomes forty-seven percentage points. Upper bound on persistent-study enrollment sets Women enrolled in persistent or chronic Lyme disease study sources to ninety percent, so Women-enrollment share, persistent-illness studies minus acute-illness studies becomes forty-eight percentage points. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

You can also move any single input yourself from the user interface and watch which results change. A sensitivity view moves one input at a time across its range while the others stay put, and ranks inputs by effect. It is an exploration of the model's own assumptions, not a measure of real-world uncertainty.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Late-stage diagnosis gap: women minus men, those draws run from minus thirty-eight point six percentage points to thirty-five point seven percentage points, with a mean of zero point three percentage points. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

There is also a cross-pollination analysis. It sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. In the full tool you tick one of each and choose the cross-pollination analysis. On the public demo there are thirteen saved comparisons to read, and this simulator appears in one of them. For example, set beside the simulator titled Long COVID Occupational Disability Cohort: cardiopulmonary exercise testing and Functional Status Findings (Mainz, Germany), on diagnostic delay the comparison marks the two as one-sided, meaning only one of the papers measures it, and on work and functional or occupational impairment it marks them as different. The numbers are copied from the simulators. The wording is written by an A I model and checked in code, and any number it cannot match is flagged. The papers differ in population, year and method, so a comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

What can you take with you, and how does it connect to other systems? The download bundle includes a formatted document, a nine-sheet Excel workbook with the parameters, data, results, evidence and limits, comma-separated files, the model's specification, and Python code that re-runs the same arithmetic. Because the results are in Excel and comma-separated files, they can be opened by statistical software and by languages such as R or Python, for further analysis. Every download records which model calls produced it. There is also a small Application Programming Interface in the full tool, which runs on the owner's computer. Another system or script can send its own assumptions to a finished simulator and get every output back at once, using the same arithmetic as the screen and rejecting values outside an input's allowed range. It can also submit papers and collect the finished simulators later. Every call needs a key made on the owner's own machine.

For health-data standards, the simulator exports each model's inputs and default outputs as a bundle in the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources standard, version R four, with the source paper and provenance. It passes the official validator for that version with no errors. It contains no patient, is not clinical data, and is a demonstration, not a certified implementation. It also demonstrates Blue Button two point oh, the interface of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services that lets a Medicare beneficiary share their own claims with an app, with their consent. In the demonstration, a person signs in against the federal sandbox, which holds only synthetic test beneficiaries. The simulator reads their coverage and claims in that same standard, reduces them to yearly totals held in memory, and shows them beside the simulator's dollar figures. Nothing is stored, and nothing is sent to an A I model. It has no access to real Medicare data and has not been through federal production review.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for two of them: diagnostic delay, medical disbelief and dismissal. The other fourteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

Finally, the potential. The concept could be transformative: in principle, the same approach can be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, turning a static report into an adjustable model that is checked against federal data and states its own gaps. That is a potential. It has been shown for those two conditions, and two papers on a different condition were run as a labeled demonstration, but no condition-specific federal evidence was curated for them, nobody has validated them, and every result still depends on what the paper itself reports. This simulator is also part of D B bun's infrastructure. The concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries, so that readers could explore the numbers in a paper instead of only reading them. That is a vision for the future, not something tested.
