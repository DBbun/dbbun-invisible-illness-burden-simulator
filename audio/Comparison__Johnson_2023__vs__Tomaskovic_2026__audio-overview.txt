This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Sex Differences in Persistent or Chronic Lyme Disease: Diagnostic Delay, Misdiagnosis, and Functional Impairment, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID Occupational Disability Cohort: cardiopulmonary exercise testing and Functional Status Findings, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This analysis compares two papers -- one Lyme disease paper and one Long COVID paper -- and does not describe either condition in general, since each group here consists of a single simulator. The Lyme disease paper documents sex-based differences in diagnostic delay, misdiagnosis with fatigue or psychiatric conditions, and self-reported functional impairment in a self-selected patient registry. The Long COVID paper documents objective, exercise-test-based work disability, sick-leave status, and myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome overlap in a severity-selected occupational-disease cohort in Germany. Neither paper reports any dollar-denominated cost figures, so no cost comparison is possible from this evidence.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers -- one on Lyme disease and one on Long COVID -- and does not describe either condition in general; each group here contains a single contributing simulator. Neither the Lyme disease paper nor the Long COVID paper reports any direct medical, non-medical, or lost-income dollar figures, so no cost comparison can be drawn from this evidence. The Lyme disease paper's evidence centers on diagnostic delay and misdiagnosis with fatigue-related or psychiatric conditions, while the Long COVID paper's evidence centers on objective exercise-test-based work disability and sick-leave status -- these are different kinds of burden evidence rather than a shared pattern.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has eight dimensions; these are the first six. On out-of-pocket or dollar cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper reports no direct medical cost, non-medical cost, or lost-income figures; its own evidence gaps state that any dollar-denominated estimate would require linkage to claims or wage data not present in this study. The Long COVID paper also reports no direct medical cost, monetized indirect cost (lost wages, productivity loss, disability payouts), or caregiver or travel cost data anywhere in the document. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On diagnostic delay. The Lyme disease paper reports that eighty-two percent of patients were diagnosed at a late stage (untreated six months or more), fifty-five percent had a diagnostic delay of three or more years, and thirty-five percent saw ten or more clinicians before diagnosis. The Long COVID paper states plainly that no data on diagnostic delay, time-to-diagnosis, or repeated care-seeking history prior to enrollment are reported in its cohort. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On work and functional or occupational impairment. The Lyme disease paper reports that thirty-five percent of patients self-identified as disabled as their primary work status (women thirty-six percent versus men thirty percent), and sixty-one percent reported eight or more bed days in the prior six months (women sixty-three percent versus men forty-nine percent). The Long COVID paper reports that an estimated sixty-one patients (sixty-six point three percent of the cohort) met an objective cardiopulmonary exercise testing-defined work-disability threshold, seventy-five percent were on medical sick leave, thirteen percent were currently working, and there was a sixty-two percentage point gap between the sick-leave rate and the working rate. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On medical disbelief, misdiagnosis, and diagnostic uncertainty. The Lyme disease paper reports that seventy-six percent of patients were misdiagnosed with chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, or a psychiatric condition before receiving a Lyme diagnosis (women seventy-eight percent versus men sixty-three percent). The Long COVID paper does not report a misdiagnosis rate; instead it describes a systemic gap in which organ-centered clinical diagnostics 'frequently fail to explain' work disability in this cohort, which is why exercise testing was used instead. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On how each paper's sample may be undercounted or diagnostically restricted. The Lyme disease paper does not carry a claims-undercounting limitation in this dataset; its stated limitation instead concerns a systemic research-design gap in how sex differences are studied. The Long COVID paper notes that every patient in its cohort was selected because they already carried the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code diagnosis code U zero nine point nine diagnosis, so its percentages reflect only already-diagnosed, occupational-disease-referral patients and would produce lower or different counts in the setting studied if applied more broadly. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On subgroup and demographic disaggregation. The Lyme disease paper breaks its diagnostic-delay, misdiagnosis, and functional-impairment figures down by sex, but its authors state the data 'did not include a sufficient sample of people across races or who identify across the gender spectrum' to analyze those subgroups. The Long COVID paper reports all of its figures (sixty-six point three percent work disability, forty-nine percent myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome prevalence, seventy-nine percent post-exertional malaise positivity, forty-eight point nine percent Bell-Score severity) as single pooled cohort numbers with no breakdown by sex, age, or race or ethnicity, despite the cohort being eighty-eight percent female. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers.

What the two papers share: Neither paper contains any direct dollar-denominated cost figures (medical, non-medical, or lost-income), even though both document substantial functional burden. Both papers caution that their specific sample should not be generalized to the broader patient population for the condition studied.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper reports dollar-denominated medical, non-medical, or indirect or lost-income costs. Neither paper reports caregiver time, paid-help, or travel costs. Neither paper's authors present their sample as representative of the general patient population for the condition studied.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, four of them here. Lyme's sex-based diagnosis gap raises a question for Long COVID? The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers check whether the path to a confirmed diagnosis or occupational-disability assessment differs by sex? Lyme's misdiagnosis gap by sex is worth asking about elsewhere. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID clinics track whether women and men are given psychiatric or fatigue-related diagnoses at different rates before a Long COVID diagnosis is reached? Long COVID used objective tests to count work disability. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could Lyme disease researchers add objective functional testing alongside self-reported impairment to assess work disability? Long COVID scored disability severity on a standard scale. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could Lyme disease researchers adopt a standardized functional-disability scale to quantify self-reported severity across patients?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an artificial intelligence model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used two model calls. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This comparison rests on a small, non-random sample: one Lyme disease simulator and one Long COVID simulator, with zero simulators in the cross-condition or other groups. It is not a controlled study and the documents were not chosen to be comparable to each other; agreement or disagreement between the two papers here reflects only what these two specific documents happened to measure, not a validated finding about either condition in general. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision.
