This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Long COVID (post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen) Case-Finding Precision and Demographic Skew Simulator.

This is a peer-reviewed methods study that built and validated a computational algorithm to identify patients with post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen from electronic health record data across fourteen hospitals and twenty community health centers in Massachusetts (Mass General Brigham system).

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulator covers only what the twenty twenty-five study measured: how precisely a computational phenotyping algorithm versus the standard diagnosis code U zero nine point nine identifies likely long COVID post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen patients from electronic health records at one Massachusetts health system (Mass General Brigham), including how demographically representative each method's identified group is.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: This study, from one Massachusetts hospital system rather than a national sample, found that relying on the official long-COVID diagnosis code alone identified far fewer patients as having lingering post-COVID symptoms than a newer computational method did, and that the people who ended up with that diagnosis code were disproportionately White and female compared with the state's overall population. One of the document's own next steps: If you have ongoing symptoms after COVID nineteen but have not received a formal long-COVID diagnosis code, this research suggests the absence of a code does not necessarily mean a lingering condition isn't real - it may be worth raising persistent symptoms with a clinician again.

For care delivery teams: Within this one Massachusetts health system, the official diagnosis code U zero nine point nine captured a smaller and more demographically skewed (whiter, more female) set of likely post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen patients than a precision-phenotyping algorithm applied to the same records, even though the code itself was reasonably precise (about seventy-eight percent of chart-reviewed diagnosis code U zero nine point nine cases were confirmed). One of the document's own next steps: Consider comparing your own system's diagnosis code U zero nine point nine diagnosis-code precision against this cohort's own chart-reviewed figure (about seventy-eight percent precision) before treating code-based counts alone as a complete picture of your long-COVID patient population.

For payers and policy audiences: In this single Massachusetts health system's electronic health records cohort, diagnosis-code-based case counts substantially understated the algorithm-estimated long-hauler population, and the code-identified group skewed toward White, female patients relative to the Massachusetts census, while the algorithm-identified group showed somewhat less skew. One of the document's own next steps: Use this study's own evidence that diagnosis-code-based counts (an estimated well under five thousand confirmed cases among six thousand three hundred forty coded patients) substantially understate the algorithm-estimated long-hauler population in the same cohort, while noting these are two structurally different case-finding methods, not simply low and high bounds on one true number.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Algorithm cohort matches overall case-cohort sex mix: Female share among algorithm-identified long-haulers from sixty-four point five percent to sixty-two point six percent. Then Female-share difference, algorithm cohort minus diagnosis code U zero nine point nine-coded cohort moves from minus three percentage points to minus four point nine percentage points. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

Now, how it works, and where machine learning and generative A I come in. You upload a document, and its text is extracted on the local machine. A small local classifier decides whether it is about Lyme disease, Long COVID, or both. It uses keyword matching and a text-similarity method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That method scores how important a word is to one document compared with all the others. This is the machine learning step. It is a classical text-matching method, not a large language model, and it makes no model call.

That decision determines which federal evidence is eligible. The simulator draws on a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units. A unit is one file from a federal dataset, such as a data table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, or one file inside a compressed archive, that has been pre-processed into a short text summary. The units come from twelve federal agencies, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the National Center for Health Statistics, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, the United States Census Bureau, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Bureau of Economic Analysis, the Health Resources and Services Administration, the Social Security Administration, and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

For this document, five hundred three units were eligible. A retrieval step scored them and picked ten as short excerpts, from these datasets: nine from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Long COVID aggregate data for twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four. Only the short summaries are read, never the raw federal files, and nothing searches the live internet while a simulator is generated.

Only then does generative A I come in. Claude Sonnet is called four times. First, it extracts the document's findings. Second, it drafts the interactive simulator and its documentation. Third, it writes named what-if scenarios and forecasts. Fourth, separately, it writes a Python version of the model that you can download. The document's extracted text, tables and figures go to the model. The original file is not uploaded. For this simulator, the four calls cost about eighty cents in total.

Then the arithmetic is done by code, not by the A I. Three kinds of figures are checked: dollar amounts, which are restated in current dollars using the consumer price index; Lyme case counts, which are compared with counts reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention; and national Long COVID prevalence percentages, which are compared with the Household Pulse Survey. If something cannot be checked, it is listed as not computed, with the reason. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Three quantities from the document were read for checking; three were compared with federal data and zero could not be compared. Raw algorithm-identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence among COVID nineteen cases (twenty twenty to twenty twenty-three): the document reports twenty-eight point five percent as the share of adults who ever had COVID nineteen that ever experienced Long COVID; the national survey's eighteen waves in that period ranged from twenty-five point six percent to thirty-five point one percent (interval bounds twenty-four point nine percent to thirty-six percent), so the document's figure is inside the published ninety-five percent interval of at least one survey wave. Positive predictive value-adjusted post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence estimate (twenty twenty to twenty twenty-three): the document reports twenty-two point eight percent as the share of adults who ever had COVID nineteen that ever experienced Long COVID; the national survey's eighteen waves in that period ranged from twenty-five point six percent to thirty-five point one percent (interval bounds twenty-four point nine percent to thirty-six percent), so the document's figure is below every survey wave interval in that period. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

The federal library can grow. In the full tool, an Administrator page lets an owner register a completely new federal resource, or re-process an existing dataset after its files are updated. This step is plain file parsing, and no A I model is called. So the evidence base can be extended with new resources, or deepened with more of the ones already there.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, positive predictive value-adjusted post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence is twenty-two point eight percent; Estimated confirmed-post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen count among diagnosis code U zero nine point nine-coded patients is four thousand nine hundred thirty-three patients; Estimated positive predictive value-adjusted long-hauler count is nineteen thousand four hundred thirty-nine patients; Female-share difference, algorithm cohort minus diagnosis code U zero nine point nine-coded cohort is minus three percentage points.

These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

You can also move any single input yourself from the user interface and watch which results change. A sensitivity view moves one input at a time across its range while the others stay put, and ranks inputs by effect. It is an exploration of the model's own assumptions, not a measure of real-world uncertainty.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For positive predictive value-adjusted post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence, those draws run from nine point nine percent to thirty-six point eight percent, with a mean of twenty point nine percent. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

There is also a cross-pollination analysis. It sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. In the full tool you tick one of each and choose the cross-pollination analysis. On the public demo there are thirteen saved comparisons to read, and this simulator appears in one of them. For example, set beside the simulator titled Lyme Disease Economic Burden Simulator (Maryland Eastern Shore, nineteen ninety-seven to two thousand), on out-of-pocket and total direct medical cost evidence the comparison marks the two as one-sided, meaning only one of the papers measures it. The numbers are copied from the simulators. The wording is written by an A I model and checked in code, and any number it cannot match is flagged. The papers differ in population, year and method, so a comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

What can you take with you, and how does it connect to other systems? The download bundle includes a formatted document, a nine-sheet Excel workbook with the parameters, data, results, evidence and limits, comma-separated files, the model's specification, and Python code that re-runs the same arithmetic. Because the results are in Excel and comma-separated files, they can be opened by statistical software and by languages such as R or Python, for further analysis. Every download records which model calls produced it. There is also a small Application Programming Interface in the full tool, which runs on the owner's computer. Another system or script can send its own assumptions to a finished simulator and get every output back at once, using the same arithmetic as the screen and rejecting values outside an input's allowed range. It can also submit papers and collect the finished simulators later. Every call needs a key made on the owner's own machine.

For health-data standards, the simulator exports each model's inputs and default outputs as a bundle in the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources standard, version R four, with the source paper and provenance. It passes the official validator for that version with no errors. It contains no patient, is not clinical data, and is a demonstration, not a certified implementation. It also demonstrates Blue Button two point oh, the interface of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services that lets a Medicare beneficiary share their own claims with an app, with their consent. In the demonstration, a person signs in against the federal sandbox, which holds only synthetic test beneficiaries. The simulator reads their coverage and claims in that same standard, reduces them to yearly totals held in memory, and shows them beside the simulator's dollar figures. Nothing is stored, and nothing is sent to an A I model. It has no access to real Medicare data and has not been through federal production review.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: undercounted by official surveillance. The other fifteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

Finally, the potential. The concept could be transformative: in principle, the same approach can be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, turning a static report into an adjustable model that is checked against federal data and states its own gaps. That is a potential. It has been shown for those two conditions, and two papers on a different condition were run as a labeled demonstration, but no condition-specific federal evidence was curated for them, nobody has validated them, and every result still depends on what the paper itself reports. This simulator is also part of D B bun's infrastructure. The concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries, so that readers could explore the numbers in a paper instead of only reading them. That is a vision for the future, not something tested.
