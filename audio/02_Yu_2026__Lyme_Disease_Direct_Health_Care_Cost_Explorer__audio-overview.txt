This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Lyme Disease Direct Health Care Cost Explorer (Claims-Based, twenty sixteen to twenty twenty-two).

This is a retrospective cohort study using a large United States claims or electronic health records dataset (Optum Market Clarity, twenty sixteen to twenty twenty-two) of seventy thousand five hundred thirty-one patients diagnosed with Lyme disease, comparing their health care costs to matched controls using four different analytic methods.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulator models only direct health care cost figures reported in a claims or electronic health records-based cohort study (Optum Market Clarity, twenty sixteen to twenty twenty-two, twenty twenty-two dollars).

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: In this large United States insurance claims dataset (twenty sixteen to twenty twenty-two, costs in twenty twenty-two dollars), the typical standardized direct medical cost per Lyme disease episode was about six hundred ninety-five dollars for localized disease versus about six thousand eight hundred thirty-three dollars for disseminated (more widely spread) disease - a difference of about six thousand one hundred thirty-eight dollars with disseminated disease costing roughly nine point eight times as much.

For care delivery teams: Among seventy thousand five hundred thirty-one Lyme disease patients identified via International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision claims codes (twenty sixteen to twenty twenty-two, twenty twenty-two dollars), standardized direct medical costs per episode were markedly higher for disseminated disease (~six thousand eight hundred thirty-three dollars) than localized disease (~six hundred ninety-five dollars), a difference of about six thousand one hundred thirty-eight dollars using the episode-based costing method.

For payers and policy audiences: This claims-based study (Optum Market Clarity, twenty sixteen to twenty twenty-two, seventy thousand five hundred thirty-one identified Lyme disease cases, costs in twenty twenty-two dollars) estimated about two hundred ninety-five thousand three hundred thirty-nine annual Lyme disease cases in sixteen high-incidence states and the District of Columbia, derived from a national case estimate (four hundred seventy-six thousand cited from a cited study twenty twenty-one), an eighty-one percent high-incidence-state share, and a zero point eight case-definition positive predictive value (cited from a cited study twenty twenty-five) - none measured directly by this study.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Self-controls cost estimate (overall): Adjusted all-cause six-month cost difference, overall from five thousand five hundred seventy-one dollars difference per patient (six months) to three thousand three hundred four dollars difference per patient (six months). Then Adjusted disseminated minus adjusted overall six-month cost difference moves from ten thousand two hundred sixty-three dollars difference per patient (six months) to twelve thousand five hundred thirty dollars difference per patient (six months). Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, seven hundred twenty-two units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from six from Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Lyme disease cost benchmarks, three from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance, one from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines. Then generative artificial intelligence, Claude Sonnet, is called five times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code. Computer code, not the artificial intelligence, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Six quantities from the document were read for checking; six were compared with federal data and zero could not be compared. Lyme disease-specific direct medical cost per episode, overall: two thousand two hundred twenty-seven in twenty twenty-two dollars is about two thousand four hundred fifty in twenty twenty-five dollars (the consumer price index all items, xone point one). The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Disseminated minus localized direct cost per episode is six thousand one hundred thirty-eight dollars difference per episode; Disseminated divided by localized direct cost per episode is nine point eight; Adjusted disseminated minus adjusted overall six-month cost difference is ten thousand two hundred sixty-three dollars difference per patient (six months); Estimated annual Lyme disease cases, high-incidence states is two hundred ninety-five thousand three hundred thirty-nine cases per year.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Self-controls cost estimate (overall) sets Adjusted all-cause six-month cost difference, overall to three thousand three hundred four dollars difference per patient (six months), so Adjusted disseminated minus adjusted overall six-month cost difference becomes twelve thousand five hundred thirty dollars difference per patient (six months). Unadjusted case-control cost estimate (overall) sets Adjusted all-cause six-month cost difference, overall to four thousand ninety-eight dollars difference per patient (six months), so Adjusted disseminated minus adjusted overall six-month cost difference becomes eleven thousand seven hundred thirty-six dollars difference per patient (six months). Self-controls cost estimate (disseminated) sets Adjusted all-cause six-month cost difference, disseminated disease to ten thousand one hundred thirty dollars difference per patient (six months), so Adjusted disseminated minus adjusted overall six-month cost difference becomes four thousand five hundred fifty-nine dollars difference per patient (six months). These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Disseminated minus localized direct cost per episode, those draws run from four thousand two hundred twenty-three dollars difference per episode to eight thousand five hundred thirty-five dollars difference per episode, with a mean of six thousand three hundred twenty dollars difference per episode. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: direct costs of care. The other fifteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an artificial intelligence model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
