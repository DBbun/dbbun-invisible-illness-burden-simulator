This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Long COVID Employment and Income Disruption: Illustrative Scenario Tool.

This is a qualitative interview study of twenty-one adults receiving care at a single academic medical center's post-COVID recovery clinic, conducted August–September twenty twenty-two, exploring how Long COVID affected their employment and well-being.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulation is based on a qualitative interview study of twenty-one adults receiving care at a single academic post-COVID recovery clinic (interviewed August–September twenty twenty-two), which described themes of reduced work capacity, job loss, insurance disruption, debt, and emotional strain but did not itself measure any dollar costs or wage losses for its participants.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: This interview study of twenty-one adults at one post-COVID recovery clinic found that Long COVID often disrupted work hours, income, and insurance coverage, and brought emotional strain such as loss of identity as a provider and fear of workplace stigma - but the study itself did not put dollar figures on any of this.

For care delivery teams: This study, drawn from twenty-one patients at a single post-COVID recovery clinic, identified recurring but unquantified themes of income loss, insurance disruption, debt accumulation, and emotional strain (loss of identity, fear of stigma), plus a practical barrier: there is no established diagnostic test for Long COVID, so accommodation requests had to rely on documented functional limitations instead.

For payers and policy audiences: This qualitative single-clinic study (sample size twenty-one) reports that Long COVID disrupted employment, income, and insurance coverage, and cites, from separate external studies, that twenty-two percent of a different sampled cohort were unable to work and forty-five percent needed a reduced schedule at six months post-infection, eighteen percent of another cohort could not return to work at one year, and a minimum of one hundred seventy billion dollars in lost United States wages was estimated for twenty twenty-two nationally (cited to Suran, the Journal of the American Medical Association twenty twenty-three) - each from a different population and method, not generated by this study's own twenty-one participants.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Higher-income earner, unable to work: Assumed annual wage before illness from fifty-five thousand dollars to one hundred thousand dollars and Share unable to return to work at one year from eighteen percent to forty percent. Then Illustrative annual income loss if unable to work moves from nine thousand nine hundred dollars to forty thousand dollars; and Illustrative annual income loss from reduced schedule moves from twelve thousand three hundred seventy-five dollars to twenty-two thousand five hundred dollars. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred fifty-one units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from six from Household Pulse Survey Long COVID data from the United States Census Bureau, two from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Long COVID aggregate data for twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four, one from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about fifty-six cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. No federal check applied to this document. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Illustrative annual income loss if unable to work is nine thousand nine hundred dollars; Illustrative annual income loss from reduced schedule is twelve thousand three hundred seventy-five dollars; Income-loss gap: unable-to-work minus reduced-schedule is minus two thousand four hundred seventy-five dollars; Cited national lost-wages total, twenty twenty-two is one hundred seventy billion dollars.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Higher-income earner, unable to work sets Assumed annual wage before illness to one hundred thousand dollars and Share unable to return to work at one year to forty percent, so Illustrative annual income loss if unable to work becomes forty thousand dollars; Illustrative annual income loss from reduced schedule becomes twenty-two thousand five hundred dollars. Low-wage worker, full income loss on reduced schedule sets Assumed annual wage before illness to twenty thousand dollars and Assumed share of income lost for someone on a reduced schedule to one, so Illustrative annual income loss if unable to work becomes three thousand six hundred dollars; Illustrative annual income loss from reduced schedule becomes nine thousand dollars. Higher reduced-schedule prevalence and loss fraction sets Share requiring reduced schedule at six months to seventy percent and Assumed share of income lost for someone on a reduced schedule to zero point eight, so Illustrative annual income loss from reduced schedule becomes twenty-eight thousand eight hundred seventy-five dollars; Income-loss gap: unable-to-work minus reduced-schedule becomes minus eighteen thousand nine hundred seventy-five dollars. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

The simulator has two forecast charts: Cumulative per-patient income loss, unable to work, assuming the annual figure repeats unchanged for three illustrative years; Cumulative per-patient income loss, reduced schedule, assuming the annual figure repeats unchanged for three illustrative years. At the default assumptions it goes from zero dollars per patient to twenty-nine thousand seven hundred dollars per patient over three years. A forecast here is a straight-line continuation of the document's stated step, not a prediction. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Illustrative annual income loss if unable to work, those draws run from eleven point six three dollars to thirty-seven thousand eight hundred fifty dollars, with a mean of eleven thousand eight hundred three dollars. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: work and income. The other fifteen are empty here. They include caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, overall quality of life, and direct costs of care. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.

Once more: this is an educational what-if model, not medical advice. It is an independent entry, not endorsed by the Department of Health and Human Services or the National Institutes of Health.
