This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Lyme Disease Economic Burden Simulator, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID Case-Finding Precision and Demographic Skew Simulator, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers: one Lyme disease paper, a historical cost-of-illness study of patients in five Maryland counties from nineteen ninety-seven to two thousand, and one Long COVID paper, a diagnostic-phenotyping methods study from one Massachusetts health system. The Lyme disease paper supplies detailed dollar-cost figures that rise with disease stage and a nationwide cost extrapolation it flags as mismatched across price years and case definitions. The Long COVID paper supplies no cost figures at all, focusing instead on how a diagnosis-code-based count compares to an algorithm-based count and on demographic skew in who gets identified. The two papers share a pattern of flagging that their own official or coded counts can produce lower or different counts than an alternative method, and both present pooled headline figures without demographic subgroup breakdown.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers - one Lyme disease paper and one Long COVID paper - and does not describe either condition in general. The Lyme disease paper provides detailed dollar-cost figures that rise with disease stage, while the Long COVID paper contains no dollar cost, productivity-loss, or access-barrier figures at all. Both papers flag that their own case-count method can produce lower or different counts than another approach in the setting studied, and both present pooled headline figures without demographic subgroup breakdown.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has seven dimensions; these are the first six. On out-of-pocket and total direct medical cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper reports a late-stage minus early-stage median total cost of five hundred twenty-six dollars (two thousand dollars), a late-to-early cost ratio of two point three, and a recomputed nationwide economic impact of one hundred ninety-four million one hundred ninety-one thousand two hundred thirty-six dollars (two thousand dollars). The Long COVID paper reports no dollar cost figures of any kind; it is a diagnostic or phenotyping methods study, not a cost-of-illness study. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On work and income loss (productivity loss). The Lyme disease paper's underlying analysis reports productivity-loss figures that differ sharply by stage and year, with late-stage productivity loss far exceeding early-stage productivity loss in its questionnaire subsample (as detailed in its own cost tables). The Long COVID paper explicitly states it contains no data on lost income, productivity, or caregiving time. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On how each paper's own case-count or diagnosis method compares to another method in the same setting. The Lyme disease paper extrapolates nationwide cost using Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-reported case counts gathered under a surveillance case definition, while its own patient cohort was identified using a different, clinical diagnosis-code-based method; the paper itself flags this as a mismatch rather than a single agreed count. The Long COVID paper shows the diagnosis code U zero nine point nine alone corresponds to an estimated confirmed-post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen count of about four thousand nine hundred thirty-three patients, while its own phenotyping algorithm estimates about nineteen thousand four hundred thirty-nine long-haulers in the same cohort - the diagnosis-code approach and the algorithm produce lower or different counts in the setting studied. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On demographic or subgroup breakdown of headline figures. The Lyme disease paper presents its pooled mean and median total-cost figures and nationwide extrapolation as single combined numbers, checking sex only for the direct-medical-cost component (finding no difference) and not for the pooled total-cost or national figures. The Long COVID paper reports its positive predictive value-adjusted prevalence figure of twenty-two point eight percent as a single Massachusetts-wide number, without breaking that specific figure down by sex, age, or race, even though organ-specific odds by sex or race or age appear elsewhere in the paper. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On precision or bias of diagnosis-code-based case identification. The Lyme disease paper does not report a precision or demographic-skew comparison between its diagnosis-code-based cohort and any alternative identification method. The Long COVID paper reports that its algorithm reaches seventy-nine point nine percent precision versus seventy-seven point eight percent for the diagnosis code U zero nine point nine, and that the algorithm-identified cohort's female share differs from the diagnosis code U zero nine point nine-coded cohort's female share by -three percentage points, indicating somewhat less demographic skew. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On structural or classification gaps affecting care or research. The Lyme disease paper does not describe a systemic classification gap of this kind; its caveats concern case-definition mismatch and small questionnaire response rather than an absent diagnostic standard. The Long COVID paper states that no scalable, universally accepted algorithm or diagnosis code exists for identifying post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen patients, and that this has hindered enrollment for large-scale clinical studies of potential therapies. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it.

What the two papers share: Each paper flags that its own official or coded case-count method can produce a lower or different count than an alternative method applied in the same setting. Each paper presents its headline figure as a single pooled number without breaking it down by demographic subgroup, a limitation each paper's own analysis notes.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper quantifies unpaid caregiving time: the Lyme disease paper asked about caregiving help but did not quantify unpaid time spent, and the Long COVID paper reports no caregiving data at all. Neither paper provides quantified insurance-coverage or access-barrier figures: the Lyme disease paper collected insurance status in its questionnaire but did not report it as a quantified outcome, and the Long COVID paper states it has no insurance or access-barrier data at all.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, four of them here. Coded diagnoses and algorithm-found cases gave very different counts. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Would applying an algorithm-based case definition to Lyme disease records, alongside the existing coded diagnoses, reveal a similar gap between coded and algorithm-identified case counts? Switching case-finding methods changed who got counted, by sex. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: If Lyme disease researchers compared different surveillance or diagnostic definitions, would the demographic makeup of who is counted shift in a similar way? Costs rose with how late the disease was caught. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: If cost data were collected for post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen patients, would those identified only through coded diagnoses show different costs than those identified through the algorithm? A nationwide cost estimate came with its own warning label. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: If Long COVID researchers attempted a nationwide cost extrapolation, would they need to flag the same kind of price-year and case-definition mismatch the Lyme disease paper warned about?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an A I model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. The limits: This analysis is based on a small, non-random sample of uploaded documents: the library counts show one Lyme disease paper, one Long COVID paper, and zero papers in the cross or other categories. It is not a controlled study and does not support general claims about either condition beyond what these two specific papers report. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.
