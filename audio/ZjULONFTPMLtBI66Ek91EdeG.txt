This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Long COVID Occupational Disability Cohort: cardiopulmonary exercise testing and Functional Status Findings (Mainz, Germany).

This is a German clinical study of ninety-two patients with long-standing, severe post-COVID nineteen condition (post-COVID condition), about half of whom also met criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome, who were undergoing evaluation for occupational disability.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulation reproduces percentages and counts reported for a single, severity-selected German occupational-disease cohort (n=ninety-two) of post-COVID nineteen condition (post-COVID condition) patients evaluated with cardiopulmonary exercise testing.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: This study followed ninety-two people with long-lasting post-COVID nineteen condition (post-COVID condition) in Germany who were being evaluated for work-related disability recognition, about half of whom also met criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome.

For care delivery teams: In this severity-selected German occupational-disease cohort (n=ninety-two), cardiopulmonary exercise testing-derived measures (VOtwopeak, peak power output) classified sixty-six point three percent of patients as meeting the study's objective work-disability threshold, and these objective measures correlated with self-reported disability (Bell Disability Scale) and with actual occupational status (seventy-five percent sick leave, thirteen percent working, twelve percent in reintegration).

For payers and policy audiences: This is a clinical or occupational-medicine study, not a cost-of-illness analysis - it reports no dollar figures.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Equalize subgroup post-exertional malaise rates: Post-exertional malaise positivity, myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome subgroup from ninety-three percent to seventy-nine percent and Post-exertional malaise positivity, non-myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome subgroup from sixty-four percent to seventy-nine percent. Then post-exertional malaise positivity gap: myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome minus non-myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome subgroup moves from twenty-nine percentage points to zero percentage points. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred thirty-eight units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from eight from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Long COVID aggregate data for twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four, one from Bureau of Labor Statistics labor cost data. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about seventy-seven cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. No federal check applied to this document. The model cited one of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Estimated patients meeting objective work-disability threshold is sixty-one patients; post-exertional malaise positivity gap: myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome minus non-myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome subgroup is twenty-nine percentage points; Occupational-status gap: sick leave minus currently-working rate is sixty-two percentage points; Self-reported moderate-to-severe disability share is forty-eight point nine percent.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Equalize subgroup post-exertional malaise rates sets Post-exertional malaise positivity, myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome subgroup to seventy-nine percent and Post-exertional malaise positivity, non-myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome subgroup to seventy-nine percent, so post-exertional malaise positivity gap: myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome minus non-myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome subgroup becomes zero percentage points. Count reintegration as working sets Share of cohort currently working to twenty-five percent, so Occupational-status gap: sick leave minus currently-working rate becomes fifty percentage points. Less severity-selected case mix sets myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome prevalence within cohort to fifteen percent, so Estimated patients meeting myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome criteria becomes thirteen point eight patients. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Estimated patients meeting objective work-disability threshold, those draws run from zero point one patients to one hundred forty-eight patients, with a mean of forty-nine point eight patients. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for two of them: work and income, overall quality of life. The other fourteen are empty here. They include caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and direct costs of care. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
