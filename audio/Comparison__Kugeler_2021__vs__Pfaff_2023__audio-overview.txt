This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Lyme Disease Diagnosis Frequency: Claims-Based Estimate versus Surveillance Counts, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID Diagnosis Code Uptake, Representation, and Care-Pattern Simulator, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers exactly two already-generated simulators - one built from a Lyme disease paper and one built from a Long COVID paper - and is not a controlled study of either condition. Both papers focus on how official diagnosis or surveillance counts compare to broader diagnosis-pattern data drawn from claims or electronic health records codes, but they measure this in different ways: the Lyme disease paper gives a specific numeric gap between a claims-based estimate and surveillance counts, while the Long COVID paper shows demographic and socioeconomic skew in who receives a diagnosis code plus a multi-year delay in that code's availability. Neither paper contains any cost, income-loss, or caregiving-burden data, so this comparison cannot address the financial burden of either condition.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers - one on Lyme disease and one on Long COVID - and does not describe either condition in general. The Lyme disease paper reports a quantified gap between a claims-based diagnosis estimate (about four hundred seventy-six thousand patients per year, ninety-five percent credible interval four hundred five thousand-five hundred forty-seven thousand) and official surveillance counts (about thirty-five thousand cases per year, with a general range of thirty thousand-forty thousand per year stated elsewhere in the paper), while the Long COVID paper instead shows a demographic and socioeconomic skew in who receives the diagnosis code U zero nine point nine rather than a comparable national count gap. Neither paper contains any direct medical cost, out-of-pocket cost, lost-income, or caregiving-burden figures, so this comparison cannot speak to the dollar cost of either condition.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has eight dimensions; these are the first six. On scale of the gap between official counts and diagnosis-pattern estimates. The Lyme disease paper estimates about four hundred seventy-six thousand patients per year were diagnosed and treated based on commercial insurance claims, compared with about thirty-five thousand cases per year reported through official surveillance (a general range of thirty thousand-forty thousand per year is also given), and states the gap reflects an unseparated mix of real underreporting and clinical overdiagnosis. The Long COVID paper does not produce a comparable national count-versus-surveillance gap figure; instead it reports that thirty-seven point two percent of the diagnosis code U zero nine point nine-coded cohort had no recorded acute COVID nineteen test, pointing to undocumented or external testing rather than a quantified national undercount multiple. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On delay between symptom onset and formal diagnostic recognition. The Lyme disease paper reports no data on diagnostic delay or time to diagnosis for individual patients. The Long COVID paper reports an approximate two-year delay between when patients began describing long COVID symptoms and when a dedicated International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code (diagnosis code U zero nine point nine) became available, which it states potentially left many earlier patients uncoded. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On demographic or socioeconomic skew in who gets diagnosed or coded. The Lyme disease paper presents its diagnosis estimates only as single pooled national figures, with no breakdown by sex, race, ethnicity, or other subgroup given in the paper itself. The Long COVID paper reports that the diagnosis code U zero nine point nine-coded cohort was eleven point three percentage points more female and ten point seven percentage points more White than the comparator group of all the COVID nineteen virus-positive patients at the same sites, and was underrepresented in higher-poverty, higher-unemployment, and higher-public-insurance-reliance zip code areas. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On out-of-pocket or direct medical cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper reports no dollar cost figures of any kind, direct or out-of-pocket. The Long COVID paper also reports no dollar cost figures of any kind, direct or out-of-pocket. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On work and income loss, and caregiving burden. The Lyme disease paper reports no data on lost income, productivity, or caregiving time. The Long COVID paper reports no data on lost income, productivity, or caregiving time. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On access barriers linked to diagnosis or coding. The Lyme disease paper notes its underlying claims data source 'might not be representative of the United States population with respect to disease risk and access to health care' but does not quantify any access barrier, and its data covers only privately insured residents under sixty-five. The Long COVID paper reports that diagnosis code U zero nine point nine-coded patients were underrepresented in areas with higher poverty, higher unemployment, and higher reliance on public health insurance, despite those areas having higher COVID nineteen case and death rates, and states patients in more socially deprived areas 'may be less likely to receive a diagnosis code U zero nine point nine code in a healthcare setting.' The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it.

What the two papers share: Both papers build their entire diagnosis-frequency evidence from administrative diagnosis or billing codes, and both explicitly state this produces lower or different counts in the setting studied. Both papers report no direct medical cost, non-medical cost, lost income, or caregiving-time figures, meaning neither can support a dollar burden estimate on its own.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper reports direct medical costs, non-medical costs, lost income, or caregiving-time data, so no dollar burden figure can be built from either paper alone. Neither paper provides a full subgroup breakdown of its core frequency or utilization figures: the Lyme disease paper presents only pooled national figures, and the Long COVID paper reports demographic skew in who is coded but does not break down its care-utilization percentages by subgroup.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, four of them here. Measuring the exact size of a diagnosis gap, not just its skew. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers calculate a similar claims-based-versus-surveillance gap number for diagnosis code U zero nine point nine diagnoses, the way the Lyme disease paper did? Checking whether diagnoses cluster in unexpected places. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Is diagnosis code U zero nine point nine coding also more common in certain regions than expected, the way claims-based Lyme diagnoses skew toward low-incidence states? Checking who actually gets the diagnosis, not just how many. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Does the Lyme disease claims-based estimate also show demographic skew by sex or race compared with surveillance-reported cases? Asking whether diagnoses come with confirmed testing. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: What proportion of the Lyme disease claims-based estimate lacks a documented confirmatory test, similar to the untested share found in Long COVID coding?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an artificial intelligence model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used three model calls. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This is a small, non-random sample based on whichever documents happened to be uploaded: exactly one Lyme disease paper and one Long COVID paper, with no cross-condition or other-category papers available, and no controlled comparison was performed. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision.
