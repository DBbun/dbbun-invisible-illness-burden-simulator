This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Persistent Lyme Disease (persistent Lyme disease or chronic Lyme disease) Care-Access Barriers Simulator.

This is a peer-reviewed survey study of one hundred fifty-five United States clinicians who treat persistent or chronic Lyme disease (persistent Lyme disease or chronic Lyme disease), conducted by LymeDisease.org in late twenty twenty-one.

What it covers, and what it does not: This models findings from a twenty twenty-one self-selected survey of one hundred fifty-five United States clinicians who treat persistent or chronic Lyme disease (persistent Lyme disease or chronic Lyme disease), covering insurance-network participation, regulatory or legal risk, and one clinician's anecdotal out-of-pocket cost range.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: The study did not measure a representative average cost for persistent Lyme disease or chronic Lyme disease care.

For care delivery teams: Among the one hundred fifty-five surveyed persistent Lyme disease or chronic Lyme disease-treating clinicians, about three-quarters reported not participating in insurance networks and about two in five reported being the subject of a medical board, insurer, or quality-improvement committee inquiry.

For payers and policy audiences: In this self-selected, non-random clinician sample, roughly three-quarters reported non-participation in insurance networks and about two in five reported a regulatory or legal inquiry (medical board, insurer, or hospital quality improvement committee).

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Lower cost, shorter duration: Clinician-quoted low end of annual out-of-pocket patient cost from ten thousand dollars to five thousand dollars and Clinician-quoted high end of annual out-of-pocket patient cost from twenty thousand dollars to sixteen thousand dollars and Assumed years of ongoing out-of-pocket persistent Lyme disease or chronic Lyme disease treatment from three years to one years. Then Midpoint of clinician-quoted annual out-of-pocket cost moves from fifteen thousand dollars to ten thousand five hundred dollars; and Gap between clinician-quoted high and low cost estimates moves from ten thousand dollars to eleven thousand dollars. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, three hundred ninety-seven units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from five from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance, two from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, two from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tick surveillance, one from National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey emergency department data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Then generative artificial intelligence, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code. Computer code, not the artificial intelligence, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Four quantities from the document were read for checking; zero were compared with federal data and four could not be compared. Low end of clinician-quoted annual out-of-pocket patient cost: not computed. The document gives no dollar value with a price year. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Midpoint of clinician-quoted annual out-of-pocket cost is fifteen thousand dollars; Gap between clinician-quoted high and low cost estimates is ten thousand dollars; Projected cumulative out-of-pocket cost over assumed years of care is forty-five thousand dollars; Share of surveyed clinicians outside insurance networks is seventy-four percent.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Lower cost, shorter duration sets Clinician-quoted low end of annual out-of-pocket patient cost to five thousand dollars and Clinician-quoted high end of annual out-of-pocket patient cost to sixteen thousand dollars and Assumed years of ongoing out-of-pocket persistent Lyme disease or chronic Lyme disease treatment to one years, so Midpoint of clinician-quoted annual out-of-pocket cost becomes ten thousand five hundred dollars; Gap between clinician-quoted high and low cost estimates becomes eleven thousand dollars. Higher cost, longer duration sets Clinician-quoted low end of annual out-of-pocket patient cost to fifteen thousand dollars and Clinician-quoted high end of annual out-of-pocket patient cost to thirty thousand dollars and Assumed years of ongoing out-of-pocket persistent Lyme disease or chronic Lyme disease treatment to ten years, so Midpoint of clinician-quoted annual out-of-pocket cost becomes twenty-two thousand five hundred dollars; Gap between clinician-quoted high and low cost estimates becomes fifteen thousand dollars. Government-plan non-participation rate sets Surveyed clinicians not participating in insurance networks to seventy-seven percent, so Share of surveyed clinicians outside insurance networks becomes seventy-seven percent. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

The simulator has one forecast chart: Cumulative out-of-pocket cost per patient, assuming the same annual estimate repeats each year. At the default assumptions it goes from zero dollars per patient to one hundred fifty thousand dollars per patient over ten years. A forecast here is a straight-line continuation of the document's stated step, not a prediction. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Midpoint of clinician-quoted annual out-of-pocket cost, those draws run from ten thousand five hundred eighty-eight dollars to twenty-two thousand one hundred fifty dollars, with a mean of sixteen thousand four hundred thirty-nine dollars. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for three of them: barriers to reaching care, administrative burden, direct costs of care. The other thirteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an artificial intelligence model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
