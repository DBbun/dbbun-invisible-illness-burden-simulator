This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: twenty twenty-two National Health Interview Survey Long COVID Prevalence Explorer.

This is a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and National Center for Health Statistics government data brief (number four hundred eighty September twenty twenty-three) reporting how many United States adults said they ever had or currently had Long COVID in twenty twenty-two, based on the National Health Interview Survey.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulation represents only self-reported 'ever had' and 'currently have' Long COVID prevalence percentages among United States adults from the twenty twenty-two National Health Interview Survey (National Center for Health Statistics Data Brief number four hundred eighty), broken out by sex, age group, and race or Hispanic origin.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: This Centers for Disease Control and Prevention survey data brief found that in twenty twenty-two, about six point nine percent of United States adults reported ever having Long COVID (symptoms lasting three or more months after a COVID nineteen infection) and three point four percent reported currently having it.

For care delivery teams: This data brief reports twenty twenty-two National Health Interview Survey prevalence of Long COVID (symptoms three or more months after COVID nineteen infection) among United States adults, broken down by sex, age, race or Hispanic origin, income, and urbanization level.

For payers and policy audiences: The twenty twenty-two National Health Interview Survey data brief estimates that six point nine percent of United States adults ever had Long COVID and three point four percent currently have it, with statistically significant higher prevalence among women versus men and notable variation by age, race or Hispanic origin, family income, and urbanization level.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Wider sex gap (illustrative bounds): Women: ever had Long COVID from eight point five percent of adults to nine point five percent of adults and Men: ever had Long COVID from five point two percent of adults to three percent of adults. Then Women's minus men's ever-prevalence moves from three point three percentage points to six point five percentage points. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred thirty-eight units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from eight from Household Pulse Survey Long COVID data from the United States Census Bureau, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Long COVID aggregate data for twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four, one from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about fifty-three cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Two quantities from the document were read for checking; two were compared with federal data and zero could not be compared. Percentage of United States adults who ever had Long COVID (twenty twenty-two): the document reports six point nine percent as the share of all United States adults that ever experienced Long COVID; the national survey's seven waves in that period ranged from fourteen percent to fourteen point eight percent (interval bounds thirteen point four percent to fifteen point four percent), so the document's figure is below every survey wave interval in that period. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Women's minus men's ever-prevalence is three point three percentage points; Ages thirty-five to forty-nine minus ages sixty-five or more ever-prevalence is four point eight percentage points; Hispanic minus Asian non-Hispanic ever-prevalence is five point seven percentage points; Current prevalence divided by ever prevalence is zero point five.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Wider sex gap (illustrative bounds) sets Women: ever had Long COVID to nine point five percent of adults and Men: ever had Long COVID to three percent of adults, so Women's minus men's ever-prevalence becomes six point five percentage points. Narrower sex gap (illustrative bounds) sets Women: ever had Long COVID to seven percent of adults and Men: ever had Long COVID to six point five percent of adults, so Women's minus men's ever-prevalence becomes zero point five percentage points. Wider age gap (illustrative bounds) sets Ages thirty-five to forty-nine: ever had Long COVID to ten percent of adults and Ages sixty-five or more: ever had Long COVID to three percent of adults, so Ages thirty-five to forty-nine minus ages sixty-five or more ever-prevalence becomes seven percentage points. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Women's minus men's ever-prevalence, those draws run from zero point seven percentage points to six point three percentage points, with a mean of three point five percentage points. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. None of this document's results is tagged to any of them, so all sixteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
