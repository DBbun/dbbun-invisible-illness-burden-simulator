This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: twenty twenty-two Long COVID Lost-Labor (Productivity) Cost Simulator.

This is a peer-reviewed research letter estimating the twenty twenty-two United States lost-labor (productivity) costs tied to Long COVID, using nationally representative Medical Expenditure Panel Survey data on full-time workers.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulation covers only lost-labor or productivity costs (missed workdays x wages, the human-capital approach) for United States full-time workers in twenty twenty-two, as reported in a single research letter using Medical Expenditure Panel Survey survey data.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: A twenty twenty-two United States study of full-time workers using a national government survey (Medical Expenditure Panel Survey) found that people who reported Long COVID (COVID nineteen symptoms lasting three months or more) had, on average, about one thousand two hundred forty-two dollarsper year more in lost-labor cost (the value of missed workdays, based on wages) than people who never had COVID nineteen, and about four hundred seventy-nine dollarsper year more than people who had COVID nineteen but did not develop Long COVID - this smaller difference only barely met the study's own statistical threshold for significance.

For care delivery teams: This twenty twenty-two national survey-based estimate shows full-time workers with Long COVID had a mean lost-labor cost about one thousand two hundred forty-two dollars per year higher than workers who never had COVID nineteen, and about four hundred seventy-nine dollars per year higher than workers who had COVID nineteen without Long COVID - the latter comparison only barely crossed the study's own significance threshold (p=zero), so it should be treated as a modest, not dramatic, distinction between these two groups.

For payers and policy audiences: Scaling the mean lost-labor cost difference between Long COVID and never-had-COVID nineteen workers to the full twenty twenty-two United States full-time workforce using the study's own formula and reported Long COVID prevalence (seven point eight percent of the study sample) yields a national estimate of roughly twelve point eight billion dollars in lost-labor costs in twenty twenty-two.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Higher prevalence & workforce sensitivity exploration: Long COVID prevalence in study sample from seven point eight percent to twenty percent and United States full-time workers, twenty twenty-two from one hundred thirty-one million nine hundred sixty thousand to one hundred fifty million. Then National twenty twenty-two lost-labor cost attributable to Long COVID moves from twelve point eight billion dollars to thirty-seven point three billion dollars. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred sixty-four units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from nine from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Long COVID aggregate data for twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about sixty-seven cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Seven quantities from the document were read for checking; seven were compared with federal data and zero could not be compared. Population-level lost labor cost attributable to Long COVID in twenty twenty-two: twelve billion seven hundred eighty-four million one hundred sixty-eight thousand six hundred seventy-five in twenty twenty-two dollars is about fourteen billion sixty-three million five hundred sixty-eight thousand four hundred nineteen in twenty twenty-five dollars (the consumer price index all items, xone point one). The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Mean lost-labor cost gap: Long COVID minus never-had-COVID nineteen is one thousand two hundred forty-two dollars per person or year; Mean lost-labor cost gap: had COVID nineteen without Long COVID minus never-had-COVID nineteen is seven hundred sixty-three dollars per person or year; Mean lost-labor cost gap: Long COVID minus had-COVID nineteen-without-Long COVID is four hundred seventy-nine dollars per person or year; National twenty twenty-two lost-labor cost attributable to Long COVID is twelve point eight billion dollars.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Higher prevalence & workforce sensitivity exploration sets Long COVID prevalence in study sample to twenty percent and United States full-time workers, twenty twenty-two to one hundred fifty million, so National twenty twenty-two lost-labor cost attributable to Long COVID becomes thirty-seven point three billion dollars. Lower prevalence & workforce sensitivity exploration sets Long COVID prevalence in study sample to two percent and United States full-time workers, twenty twenty-two to one hundred million, so National twenty twenty-two lost-labor cost attributable to Long COVID becomes two point five billion dollars. Equal paid-sick-leave access sets Paid sick leave prevalence, Long COVID group to eighty-one point two percent, so Paid sick leave prevalence gap: overall sample minus Long COVID group becomes zero percentage points. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

The simulator has one forecast chart: Cumulative excess lost-labor cost per Long COVID worker if this annual gap repeated unchanged for five years. At the default assumptions it goes from zero dollars per person to six thousand two hundred ten dollars per person over five years. A forecast here is a straight-line continuation of the document's stated step, not a prediction. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Mean lost-labor cost gap: Long COVID minus never-had-COVID nineteen, those draws run from seven hundred twenty-one dollars per person or year to two thousand one hundred eighty-six dollars per person or year, with a mean of one thousand four hundred fifty-eight dollars per person or year. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: work and income. The other fifteen are empty here. They include caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, overall quality of life, and direct costs of care. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.

Once more: this is an educational what-if model, not medical advice. It is an independent entry, not endorsed by the Department of Health and Human Services or the National Institutes of Health.
