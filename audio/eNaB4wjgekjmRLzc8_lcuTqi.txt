This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Persistent Lyme Disease Trial-Eligibility and Diagnostic-Burden Explorer.

This peer-reviewed study uses a four thousand one hundred eighty-three-patient Lyme disease registry (MyLymeData) to show that commonly used clinical-trial eligibility criteria exclude roughly nine out of ten persistent Lyme disease patients, far more than the ~thirty-five percent typical exclusion seen in other diseases cited by the authors.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulator reproduces stepwise clinical-trial eligibility yields and diagnostic or functional burden comparisons reported in a twenty twenty-five peer-reviewed analysis of the MyLymeData patient registry (persistent Lyme disease [persistent Lyme disease] versus acute Lyme disease [acute Lyme disease] patients).

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: In this patient registry, people who identified as having persistent Lyme disease were far more likely than those with acute Lyme disease to report having been given a different diagnosis first (such as chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, or a psychiatric condition) and to report being unable to work due to disability.

For care delivery teams: Among registry participants, patients identifying as having persistent Lyme disease showed substantially higher rates of prior misdiagnosis (chronic fatigue syndrome or fibromyalgia, psychiatric conditions), diagnosed coinfections, and self-reported work disability than those with acute Lyme disease.

For payers and policy audiences: This registry-based analysis found that sequentially applying standard clinical-trial eligibility criteria reduces the recruitable persistent Lyme disease sample from about sixty-four percent to roughly ten percent, compared with a cited ~sixty-five percent average yield in trials for other diseases (not Lyme-specific).

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Loosen chronic fatigue syndrome or fibromyalgia syndrome & coinfection exclusions: Retention fraction after excluding prior chronic fatigue syndrome or fibromyalgia misdiagnosis from zero point six to one and Retention fraction after excluding diagnosed coinfections from zero point three to one. Then Compounded persistent Lyme disease trial-eligible yield moves from ten point two percent to sixty-four percent; and persistent Lyme disease trial yield minus other-disease benchmark yield moves from minus fifty-four point eight percentage points to minus one percentage points. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, three hundred eighty-four units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from five from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance, three from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, two from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tick surveillance. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about seventy-seven cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. No federal check applied to this document. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Compounded persistent Lyme disease trial-eligible yield is ten point two percent; Prior-misdiagnosis gap, persistent Lyme disease minus acute Lyme disease is thirty-one percentage points; Work-disability share gap, persistent Lyme disease minus acute Lyme disease is twenty-two percentage points; persistent Lyme disease trial yield minus other-disease benchmark yield is minus fifty-four point eight percentage points.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Loosen chronic fatigue syndrome or fibromyalgia syndrome & coinfection exclusions sets Retention fraction after excluding prior chronic fatigue syndrome or fibromyalgia misdiagnosis to one and Retention fraction after excluding diagnosed coinfections to one, so Compounded persistent Lyme disease trial-eligible yield becomes sixty-four percent; persistent Lyme disease trial yield minus other-disease benchmark yield becomes minus one percentage points. Narrow the misdiagnosis-rate gap sets persistent Lyme disease patients misdiagnosed before Lyme diagnosis to fifty-five percent and acute Lyme disease patients misdiagnosed before Lyme diagnosis to fifty-four percent, so Prior-misdiagnosis gap, persistent Lyme disease minus acute Lyme disease becomes one percentage points. Narrow the disability-rate gap sets persistent Lyme disease patients self-reporting disabled work status to fifteen percent and acute Lyme disease patients self-reporting disabled work status to fourteen percent, so Work-disability share gap, persistent Lyme disease minus acute Lyme disease becomes one percentage points. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Compounded persistent Lyme disease trial-eligible yield, those draws run from one point seven percent to fifty-eight point eight percent, with a mean of nineteen point six percent. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for two of them: diagnostic delay, work and income. The other fourteen are empty here. They include caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, overall quality of life, and direct costs of care. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
