This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Long COVID Phenotyping versus Diagnosis-Code Surveillance Gap Explorer.

This is a peer-reviewed cohort study (the Journal of the American Medical Association Network Open, twenty twenty-six) that used a validated A I phenotyping algorithm (PtwoRC) on electronic health record data from four hundred fifty-seven thousand nine hundred fifty COVID nineteen patients across fifty-eight hospitals in four United States regions (New England, Southeast Texas, Southern California, Western Pennsylvania) to estimate how many people actually develop Long COVID (post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen), compared with how many are captured by standard diagnostic coding.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulator reflects only what a twenty twenty-six the Journal of the American Medical Association Network Open electronic health records cohort study (four hundred fifty-seven thousand nine hundred fifty COVID nineteen patients, fifty-eight hospitals, four United States regions, Qtwo twenty twenty-Qtwo twenty twenty-four) reported: A I phenotyping-identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen (Long COVID) prevalence, chronic-condition prevalence, a cited diagnosis-code surveillance benchmark, and the study's own cautious national and decade-ahead projections.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: This study used A I analysis of electronic health records across four United States regions to look for Long COVID symptoms, and found it in about sixteen percent of COVID nineteen patients overall (ranging thirteen point six percent-twenty-two point seven percent by region) - more than double what standard diagnosis coding typically captures (under seven percent).

For care delivery teams: Phenotyping algorithms applied to this four-region electronic health records cohort identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen in roughly double the share of COVID nineteen patients that standard diagnosis coding typically captures, and found that about eighty-nine percent of identified cases involved an ongoing chronic condition (fourteen point five percent of all COVID nineteen patients in the cohort).

For payers and policy audiences: This cohort study found a large gap between A I-phenotyping-identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence (sixteen point three percent overall, thirteen point six percent-twenty-two point seven percent regionally) and the diagnosis-code-based surveillance benchmark it cites (under seven percent, drawn from external studies), with fourteen point five percent of all COVID nineteen patients in the cohort developing a chronic condition.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Southern California region: Phenotyping-identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence from sixteen point three percent to twenty-two point seven percent. Then Phenotyping-identified prevalence minus code-based surveillance rate moves from nine point three percentage points to fifteen point seven percentage points; and Chronic conditions as a share of identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen patients moves from eighty-nine point three percent to sixty-four point one percent. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred ninety units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from nine from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Long COVID aggregate data for twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about seventy-six cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Two quantities from the document were read for checking; two were compared with federal data and zero could not be compared. Phenotyping-identified cumulative post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence among COVID nineteen patients (overall, four-region cohort) (twenty twenty to twenty twenty-four): the document reports sixteen point three percent as the share of adults who ever had COVID nineteen that ever experienced Long COVID; the national survey's twenty-seven waves in that period ranged from twenty-five point six percent to thirty-five point one percent (interval bounds twenty-four point nine percent to thirty-six percent), so the document's figure is below every survey wave interval in that period. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Phenotyping-identified prevalence minus code-based surveillance rate is nine point three percentage points; National chronic-post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen projection is fourteen million nine hundred seventy-six thousand two hundred individuals; Chronic conditions as a share of identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen patients is eighty-nine point three percent; Illustrative projected future chronic post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence is twenty-one point four percent.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Southern California region sets Phenotyping-identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence to twenty-two point seven percent, so Phenotyping-identified prevalence minus code-based surveillance rate becomes fifteen point seven percentage points; Chronic conditions as a share of identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen patients becomes sixty-four point one percent. Western Pennsylvania region sets Phenotyping-identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence to thirteen point six percent, so Phenotyping-identified prevalence minus code-based surveillance rate becomes six point six percentage points; Chronic conditions as a share of identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen patients becomes one hundred seven percent. Population-level code capture sets Diagnosis-code-based surveillance rate to one percent, so Phenotyping-identified prevalence minus code-based surveillance rate becomes fifteen point three percentage points. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Phenotyping-identified prevalence minus code-based surveillance rate, those draws run from seven point four percentage points to twenty-one point three percentage points, with a mean of fourteen point one percentage points. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: undercounted by official surveillance. The other fifteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
