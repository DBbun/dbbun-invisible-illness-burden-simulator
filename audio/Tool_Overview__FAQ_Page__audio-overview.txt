This is an educational what-if tool, not medical advice.

This audio overview walks through the Frequently Asked Questions page of the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The page has thirty-five questions in five groups, with a table of contents at the top that links to each one, and a link back to the list after every answer. This recording follows the groups in order, so you know what is there and where to look.

The page is a general reference for how the tool works and how to read what it produces. When you are looking at one specific simulator, its own Operation Flow tab shows that simulator's real numbers at each step.

The first group is Start here. It opens with the question, what can I do with this, and what is it worth? The answer: the tool turns a published paper on Lyme disease or Long COVID, or up to five related documents, into a working what-if model that can be checked. You can see what a paper measured, what it only assumed, and what its results do not cover. You can change an assumption and watch every result move. You can check the paper's figures against federal data, which is done in code and not by the artificial intelligence model, and the result is shown even when the figures do not match. You can compare a Lyme paper with a Long COVID paper side by side. You can switch the wording for patients and advocates, care teams, or payers and policy teams, while the numbers stay the same. And you can take the work with you as a P D F, a nine-sheet Excel workbook, C S V files, Python code and a prototype export in the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources standard.

Why does it matter? A cost-of-illness paper is normally a static document. A patient cannot ask what happens if costs are thirty percent higher, and the burdens a paper never measured are invisible. The tool makes those visible and shows where each number came from. What it is not: it is not medical advice, it never recommends or ranks treatments, and its results describe published studies, not what will happen to any one person.

Also in this group is a short entry on who built the tool, with a verified biography of Dr. Kartoun and a statement that this is an independent entry, not endorsed by the Department of Health and Human Services, the National Institutes of Health, or any past employer, funder or challenge host. There is a step-by-step walkthrough that changes one assumption in one real study. There is an answer to the question, is this just a simulator, what makes it different? The difference is evidence labels on every number, federal checks computed by ordinary code, a map of burdens with no tagged result, comparisons across the two conditions, three readings of the same evidence, provenance and portability, and a repeatable pipeline with its own tests. And there is the difference between this read-only public demonstration and the full tool. In the demonstration you can open every simulator, change assumptions, read every tab and comparison, and download trimmed bundles. Generating a new simulator from your own document, deleting, the Administrator tab and the programming interface run in the full tool on Dr. Kartoun's computer. This entry also shows real screens from the full tool.

The second group is Using the library. It explains how to read the library map: twenty-five simulators by sixteen burden categories. A colored cell means the simulator has at least one result for that burden. A blank cell is a gap in what that simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent. And it explains that the switch labeled Read this simulator as changes only the wording, never the numbers.

The third group is Using each tab of a simulator. Overview shows the title, an at-a-glance strip, the original source with links, a plain-language summary, which assumptions move the results most, the burden map, and, where a paper reports it, who bears the cost. Operation Flow shows what happened to your document. Explore is where the what-ifs work. An input is one number the model starts from, labeled observed, assumed or projected, with its range. A named scenario is a ready-made combination of changed inputs with a one-sentence goal. The artificial intelligence model proposes scenarios, but code applies and calculates them, and they are labeled illustrative. Forecast projects a result forward only where a paper reports a per-period rate, so many simulators correctly have no forecast. Sensitivity moves one input at a time to show how far each result moves. Evidence and Sources is the audit, including how to read the chart that compares the paper's numbers with federal numbers. Export lists exactly what you can download.

The fourth group is Comparing Lyme disease and Long COVID. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. The numbers are copied from the simulators. The wording is written by an artificial intelligence model and checked in code. Because the two papers differ in population, year and method, the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The fifth and largest group is How it works and how far to trust it. It begins with which federal resources are the most valuable. The ranking shows that value is concentrated in a few small, checkable sources, such as Centers for Disease Control and Prevention surveillance, the consumer price index, a health-center visit survey and a Long COVID prevalence survey, rather than in the size of the library. And being shown to the model is not the same as being used: only a computed cross-check proves a federal number was compared with the document.

It then explains how the simulator works: the document is read, a local classifier picks the condition, relevant federal evidence is retrieved, four calls to the artificial intelligence model run in sequence, and code validates the model, runs the federal checks and builds the downloads. Nothing searches the live internet when a simulator is generated. There is a diagram of what the paper, the federal data and the simulator each add alone, and what they reveal together. There are definitions of observed, assumed and projected, and a guide to the sixteen burden categories, including which categories carry a marker because people with lived experience described or confirmed them.

The page then describes how real people shaped the tool, by role only and never by name: patient advocates, a Long COVID patient, a government deputy director, and people with Lyme disease who replied on a public discussion board. Each group points to what changed in the product. It also answers whether the tool recommends or evaluates treatments. It does not. And it explains which artificial intelligence model is used, that the model can be changed in the full tool, and that code does every calculation whichever model is chosen.

There is an answer on the programming interface: the full tool can run a finished simulator with your own assumptions, or accept papers in a queue, from another system, with a key created on your own machine. There is a detailed answer on the role federal data plays. The simulator's numbers come from the uploaded document. Federal data decides what is eligible, is offered as background, is used in computed cross-checks, and is shown as benchmarks. The limits are stated: retrieval is keyword-based, only three kinds of figures are cross-checked, some coverage gaps are named, the data are a downloaded snapshot refreshed by hand, and claims-based sources undercount.

The page explains how the tool relates to federal health-data standards. The export passes the official validator with no errors, but it is a demonstration with no patient and no certification. Blue Button 2.0 is demonstrated against the federal sandbox with synthetic beneficiaries only. The tool does not connect to any real health-record network, and it says plainly that the United States Core Data for Interoperability Plus and the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement are not implemented.

It answers whether the tool works for conditions other than Lyme disease and Long COVID. It was built and checked for those two. Two papers on another condition were run through the same pipeline as a labeled demonstration. No condition-specific federal evidence was curated for them, and nobody has validated the results.

It lists the limitations, which it calls Gaps and Boundaries: four checks run against every document and flagged only when the document itself supports them. These are claims and diagnosis-code undercounting, subgroup disaggregation, a funding gap, and a systemic gap in the care or research system. It also states what the simulators do not model, such as household burden and care that a paper does not price.

And it answers the questions of trust. What happens to my uploaded document? The original file is never sent to the model provider. Only extracted text, table captions and figures are sent, no generated code runs on Dr. Kartoun's machine, and every model call is recorded. Can I trust the numbers? Every number traces to the document, a named federal source, or a labeled assumption. Is the text written by artificial intelligence? Yes, and the page says so, including that the provider applies a statistical watermark to model text from the thirtieth of September, twenty twenty-six. And what does the artificial intelligence model already know on its own? It probably saw many public papers during training and has a cutoff date, so the tool never treats the model's background knowledge as evidence.

If you take one idea away from this page, let it be this: every number traces back to the paper, a named federal source, or a labeled assumption, and the tool states its own gaps.

Once more: this is an educational what-if tool, not medical advice. Thank you for listening.
