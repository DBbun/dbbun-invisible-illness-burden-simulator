This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Long COVID and Financial Hardship Risk (twenty twenty-two Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System).

This peer-reviewed research brief uses twenty twenty-two Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System survey data on two hundred seventy-one thousand seventy-six United States adults (age eighteen or more, forty states plus the District of Columbia) to ask whether long COVID is associated with financial hardship - specifically food insecurity, inability to pay bills, and threat of losing utility service - and whether this association differs by income and education level.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulator models associations reported in a single cross-sectional twenty twenty-two Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System survey (two hundred seventy-one thousand seventy-six United States adults, forty states + the District of Columbia) between self-reported long COVID (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-based definition, symptoms ≥three months) and three financial-hardship outcomes: food insecurity, inability to pay bills, and being threatened with loss of utility service.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: In this twenty twenty-two national survey, adults who reported long COVID symptoms lasting three months or more had a higher chance - several percentage points higher - of facing food insecurity, trouble paying bills, or a utility shutoff threat, compared with similar people without long COVID in the same income or education group.

For care delivery teams: Across income and education strata, long COVID was associated with a higher probability of three financial-hardship outcomes - food insecurity, bill non-payment, and utility-shutoff threat - with the largest increases generally seen in lower-income and lower-education groups.

For payers and policy audiences: This twenty twenty-two Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System analysis found long COVID associated with elevated probability of food insecurity, bill non-payment, and utility-shutoff threat across nearly all income and education strata, with effects generally strongest among lower-income households.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Upper-bound income hardship estimate: Food insecurity average marginal effect, pooled across income groups from four percentage points to seven percentage points and Inability-to-pay-bills average marginal effect, pooled across income groups from five percentage points to eight percentage points and Utility-shutoff-threat average marginal effect, pooled across income groups from four percentage points to six percentage points. Then Combined hardship-probability increase, income groups moves from thirteen percentage points to twenty-one percentage points; and Portion of bill-payment-hardship increase linked to lost work hours moves from zero point seven percentage points to one percentage points. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred fifty-one units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from eight from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Long COVID aggregate data for twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four, one from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about sixty-five cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Three quantities from the document were read for checking; three were compared with federal data and zero could not be compared. United States adults who ever experienced long COVID (cited from National Center for Health Statistics Household Pulse Survey, not this study's own sample) (twenty twenty-four): the document reports seventeen point six percent as the share of all United States adults that ever experienced Long COVID; the national survey's nine waves in that period ranged from seventeen point four percent to eighteen point four percent (interval bounds sixteen point nine percent to eighteen point nine percent), so the document's figure is inside the published ninety-five percent interval of at least one survey wave. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Combined hardship-probability increase, income groups is thirteen percentage points; Combined hardship-probability increase, education groups is eleven percentage points; Portion of bill-payment-hardship increase linked to lost work hours is zero point seven percentage points; Income-group minus education-group bill-payment average marginal effect is zero percentage points.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Upper-bound income hardship estimate sets Food insecurity average marginal effect, pooled across income groups to seven percentage points and Inability-to-pay-bills average marginal effect, pooled across income groups to eight percentage points and Utility-shutoff-threat average marginal effect, pooled across income groups to six percentage points, so Combined hardship-probability increase, income groups becomes twenty-one percentage points; Portion of bill-payment-hardship increase linked to lost work hours becomes one percentage points. Lower-bound income hardship estimate sets Food insecurity average marginal effect, pooled across income groups to one percentage points and Inability-to-pay-bills average marginal effect, pooled across income groups to two percentage points and Utility-shutoff-threat average marginal effect, pooled across income groups to one percentage points, so Combined hardship-probability increase, income groups becomes four percentage points; Portion of bill-payment-hardship increase linked to lost work hours becomes zero point three percentage points. Upper-bound education hardship estimate sets Food insecurity average marginal effect, pooled across education groups to four percentage points and Inability-to-pay-bills average marginal effect, pooled across education groups to six percentage points and Utility-shutoff-threat average marginal effect, pooled across education groups to four percentage points, so Combined hardship-probability increase, education groups becomes fourteen percentage points; Income-group minus education-group bill-payment average marginal effect becomes minus one percentage points. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Combined hardship-probability increase, income groups, those draws run from five point eight percentage points to nineteen point seven percentage points, with a mean of twelve point four percentage points. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. None of this document's results is tagged to any of them, so all sixteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
