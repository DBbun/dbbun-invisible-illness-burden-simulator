This audio overview walks through the supporting documents for the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. They are dated October twelfth, twenty twenty-six, and were prepared by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts, for the Cost of Illness track of the Top X Tech Sprint for Artificial Intelligence and Invisible Illness, run by the United States Department of Health and Human Services. It is an independent entry. It is not endorsed by the Department of Health and Human Services or the National Institutes of Health.

There are five documents, each one page: a feedback log, a quality assurance log, an interoperability status table, a policy brief, and a reference list. They are public, and they are linked from the demonstration's FAQ page. This recording covers what each one says, so you know what is in them and what they do and do not claim.

The first document is Dr. Kartoun's feedback log, which lists the feedback he received and the changes he made. Since the August thirteenth kickoff, Dr. Kartoun showed the work at the sprint demonstrations and office hours and changed the product after each round. He held eight interviews. Six were with patient advocates from the list the sprint organizers provided for user research. He arranged the other two himself, with a Long COVID patient and with a deputy director at a Massachusetts government agency. He also asked seventeen people with Lyme disease on the LymeX Healthathon board a question each, and fifteen replied. People are described by role, never by name.

The log is a table. Each row says who gave the feedback, what was heard, what Dr. Kartoun changed, and where to see the change. A federal lead asked which federal resources matter most, and a ranked list of federal sources with the reason for each ranking was added. Federal leads asked for a variety of what-if scenarios, and the tool now has named scenarios, sensitivity charts, and, on the five simulators whose papers report a per-period rate, a forecast band showing the middle eighty percent of five hundred random draws, labeled as illustrative. A Long COVID patient and patient advocates described how claims and diagnosis codes miss real cases, and a guardrail now flags figures that come from claims or International Classification of Diseases codes as likely undercounts. A state health-policy official needed structured data for budget forecasting, and an Excel export was added. It holds saved values and formula text, not working formulas, so it does not recalculate. Federal reviewers asked about time periods and real versus nominal dollars, and price-year and payer labels were added. Some requests were heard and not built, such as a household-income caveat and a two-way process for flagging assumptions that do not match reality, and the log says so plainly.

The first page also states that feedback does not imply that anyone endorses the product, and that people who gave feedback are not authors, co-developers or sponsors of it.

The second document is the quality assurance log. It lists how quality was checked. The automated test suite has two hundred twenty-eight passing tests, and five more are intentionally skipped because they cover an older two-model workflow that was retired. They are not failures. Several artificial intelligence tools critiqued earlier versions of every simulator and comparison, and the issues they raised were fixed once in the code and prompts, then all twenty-five simulators were regenerated. A separate artificial intelligence code review, by GPT-5 Codex, produced ten findings. Nine were fixed with regression tests, and one, a single-user limit on the browser token, is documented.

For federal cross-checks, the log is specific. Twelve of the twenty-five simulators have at least one computed federal operation, and ten compare a figure with federal data. Other figures may receive context only. The Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources export passes the official validator with no errors on twenty-six files: one for each of the twenty-five library simulators, plus one for a reuse demonstration. The second reuse demonstration has no validator record. The check looked at structure only. It did not check the codes against an online code library, because the codes in these files are DBbun's own labels, such as observed, assumed and projected, which no standard library contains, and because no file contents needed to leave the computer. The downloadable Python file in each library bundle gave the same answer as the web page on five hundred ninety-three result values and twelve forecast series.

The log also says what it does not show. The critiques covered earlier versions. The spoken overviews use a synthetic voice, and the log says how their scripts were checked. It also names the tools: Claude Sonnet 5 inside the product, Claude Code with Claude Sonnet 5.5 for building and testing, Kokoro text-to-speech for the voice, and Grok, Gemini and GPT models for reviews. Dr. Kartoun designed and directed the simulator. Artificial intelligence tools wrote the code and text under his continuous review, feedback and modification.

The third document is the interoperability status. It is a table with one row per standard, and each row says what DBbun does today, what it does not do, the evidence, and the next step. The Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources export, release four, contains no patient, is not clinical data, and is not certified. For the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Interoperability Framework, the tool echoes its consent and transparency principles at its own scale and claims no formal alignment. Blue Button two point zero is demonstrated only against the federal sandbox with synthetic beneficiaries. A real run on October seventh read one hundred fifty-five synthetic claims. It is not production access, and it is not endorsed by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. The programming interface runs on Dr. Kartoun's computer, not as a public service. For reuse, two papers about a collapsed lung, which is not one of the two conditions the tool was built for, were run through the same pipeline as labeled demonstrations. One studies chronic pain and numbness after keyhole surgery for a collapsed lung. The other is a cost-effectiveness analysis of emergency department care for a collapsed lung. They have no curated federal evidence and no outside validation.

The fourth document is a policy brief titled Six burdens with no tagged result in a library of twenty-five Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators. A numeric result is a number that a simulator calculates, such as a gap between two rates, a share of patients, or an amount per episode. Each one can carry a tag, which is a label placing it in one of sixteen burden categories. A tag is only a category label; it does not by itself establish that the study measured the burden. For example, in the simulator built from the MyLymeData chartbook, the thirty-two point gap in misdiagnosis rates between persistent and acute Lyme disease is tagged diagnostic delay, and the fifty percent of patients whose physician does not accept their insurance is tagged barriers to reaching care. In the Lyme claims study, the extra direct cost of disseminated disease, six thousand one hundred thirty-eight dollars per episode, is tagged direct costs of care. In a Long COVID lost-labor study, the extra yearly lost-labor amount of about twelve hundred forty-two dollars per person is tagged work and income. Here is one more example for each of the other categories. For undercounting by official surveillance, a claims-based estimate of about four hundred seventy-eight thousand diagnosed and treated Lyme disease cases a year. For overall quality of life, forty-nine percent of patients in a Long COVID cohort reporting moderate-to-severe disability. For administrative burden, thirty-nine percent of surveyed clinicians reported to a medical board, an insurer or a quality-improvement committee. For harm from treatment, an adverse-event rate of about nineteen percent in one antibiotic trial. For medical disbelief and dismissal, a fifteen-point gap between women and men in being misdiagnosed as having a different condition. And for uncoordinated, fragmented care, an illustrative count of six possible communication links among the specialist types one patient sees, a figure that is illustrative and not one the study reports. Across the twenty-five simulators, sixty-seven numeric results were tagged. Forty-nine of them, seventy-three percent, fall in three categories: work and income, direct costs of care, and undercounting by official surveillance. Six categories have no tagged result for either condition: care continuity risk, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, congenital or parent-to-child transmission, longer-term opportunity costs, and psychological burden. For example, measuring them could mean: the share of a patient's care that depends on one person, for care continuity risk; caregiver hours per week, for caregiving; task completion time or the share needing help, for cognitive impact; the transmission rate or the number of children affected, for parent-to-child transmission; years of schooling disrupted or tutoring hours, for longer-term opportunity costs; and the prevalence of anxiety or depression, for psychological burden. The brief is careful about what that means. It shows where this library's numbers are thin. It does not show that those burdens are small, or that they were never studied elsewhere.

The brief offers one suggestion, labeled as the author's suggestion and not a finding: ask whether an existing federal survey could add an item on caregiver hours per week and one on years of schooling disrupted. The other four categories would need a review of clinical relevance and feasibility first. It also says how far to trust the counts. Dr. Kartoun chose the papers, not through a systematic review. An artificial intelligence model assigned each tag, and code checked the tags against a fixed list.

The fifth document is the reference list. It lists the published source of each of the twenty-five simulators, with a link and a PubMed identifier where one exists. Ten of them carry a dagger, which is a small cross-shaped footnote mark. Those are the ten references on the reference slide of the organizers' August thirteenth kickoff presentation, and all ten have a simulator in this library. Being on that list does not imply endorsement. The list also gives the two papers used for the reuse demonstrations, and an inventory of the federal datasets and standards the documents name, with their publishers and periods.

If you read only one thing in these documents, read the limits. Each one says what the work shows, what it does not show, and who did what. That is deliberate. The aim is a tool whose numbers can be inspected and whose gaps are visible.

Once more: this is an educational what-if tool, not medical advice. Thank you for listening.
