This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Post-COVID nineteen Excess Direct Medical Cost Explorer (Privately Insured, twenty twenty Cohort).

This Centers for Disease Control and Prevention study used a large United States commercial insurance claims database (IQVIA PharMetrics Plus) to compare medical spending, starting thirty-one days after diagnosis, between people diagnosed with COVID nineteen (April-August twenty twenty) and matched people without a COVID nineteen diagnosis.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulation reproduces the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention or the twenty twenty-three study comparison of total direct medical claims costs (combined insurer- and patient-paid amounts) between privately insured people diagnosed with COVID nineteen in April-August twenty twenty and matched people without a diagnosis, over the six months starting thirty-one days after diagnosis.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: A twenty twenty-three Centers for Disease Control and Prevention study compared medical claims costs for privately insured children and adults diagnosed with COVID nineteen in twenty twenty (before vaccines existed) to similar people without a COVID nineteen diagnosis.

For care delivery teams: This Centers for Disease Control and Prevention claims-based analysis found that both pediatric and adult COVID nineteen patients had significantly higher six-month downstream medical costs than matched non-COVID controls, with hospitalized patients showing dramatically higher subsequent costs (about threex for adults, about ninex for children) than non-hospitalized patients with a COVID nineteen diagnosis.

For payers and policy audiences: This Centers for Disease Control and Prevention study provides age- and hospitalization-stratified estimates of added near-term direct medical claims costs associated with a COVID nineteen diagnosis in a privately insured, under-sixty-five pre-vaccine twenty twenty cohort, described by the authors as a likely lower-bound estimate of post-COVID nineteen condition (post-COVID condition)-related cost because no post-COVID condition-specific diagnosis code existed at the time.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Older adults (fifty-sixty-four): Adult excess direct medical cost, six months from one thousand five hundred sixty-two dollars to two thousand one hundred ninety-one dollars. Then Adults' six-month excess direct medical cost moves from one thousand five hundred sixty-two dollars to two thousand one hundred ninety-one dollars. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred fifty-one units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from seven from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from Bureau of Labor Statistics labor cost data, one from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Long COVID aggregate data for twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about sixty-nine cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Six quantities from the document were read for checking; six were compared with federal data and zero could not be compared. Excess direct medical cost, children, six months post-acute (cases versus matched controls): one thousand eleven in twenty twenty dollars is about one thousand two hundred fifty-eight in twenty twenty-five dollars (the consumer price index all items, xone point two). The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Children's six-month excess direct medical cost is one thousand eleven dollars; Adults' six-month excess direct medical cost is one thousand five hundred sixty-two dollars; Adult male minus female excess cost at six months is two hundred thirty-four dollars; Adult hospitalization six-month cost multiplier is three point two.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Older adults (fifty-sixty-four) sets Adult excess direct medical cost, six months to two thousand one hundred ninety-one dollars, so Adults' six-month excess direct medical cost becomes two thousand one hundred ninety-one dollars. Younger adults (eighteen-twenty-four) sets Adult excess direct medical cost, six months to eight hundred ninety-two dollars, so Adults' six-month excess direct medical cost becomes eight hundred ninety-two dollars. High hospitalization cost ratio (illustrative) sets Adult hospitalized-versus-non-hospitalized cost ratio, six months to ten, so Adult hospitalization six-month cost multiplier becomes ten. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Children's six-month excess direct medical cost, those draws run from zero point nine nine dollars to two thousand nine hundred eighty-seven dollars, with a mean of one thousand four hundred fifty-eight dollars. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: direct costs of care. The other fifteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
