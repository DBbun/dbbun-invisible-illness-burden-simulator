This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Persistent Lyme Disease Trial-Eligibility and Diagnostic-Burden Explorer, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID Symptom Patterns and Self-Reported Work or Economic Impact, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers - one on Lyme disease and one on Long COVID - and is not a controlled study comparing the conditions themselves. The Lyme disease paper focuses on diagnostic delay, misdiagnosis, work disability, and clinical-trial eligibility barriers using a patient registry, while the Long COVID paper tracks symptom persistence, work-productivity scores, and self-reported economic condition over thirty-six months in a Japanese hospitalized cohort. Both papers show work-related burden but measure it with different, non-convertible metrics, and neither reports any dollar-denominated cost figures. Both also pool their findings without demographic subgroup breakdowns.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers only - one Lyme disease paper and one Long COVID paper - so it describes these two papers, not the conditions in general. Both papers show measurable work-related burden, but the Lyme disease paper measures it as a percentage of patients self-reporting as disabled while the Long COVID paper measures it with a presenteeism score and a separate percentage reporting worsened economic condition, so the two figures cannot be directly compared. Neither paper reports direct medical costs, non-medical costs, or dollar-denominated indirect costs, and both pool their burden findings without breaking them down by demographic subgroup.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has seven dimensions; these are the first six. On work and income loss or employment burden. The Lyme disease paper reports that twenty-eight percent of persistent Lyme disease patients self-reported as 'disabled' in work status compared with six percent of acute Lyme disease patients, used as a proxy for lost earning capacity rather than a dollar figure. The Long COVID paper reports a presenteeism score of sixty (World Health Organization Health and Work Performance Questionnaire scale) for patients in the continuous symptom-trajectory category, and that forty-three point six percent of patients in that category reported their economic condition had 'become very bad' or worsened, measured as self-reported scores rather than dollar figures. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On diagnostic delay. The Lyme disease paper reports that only nine percent of persistent Lyme disease patients were diagnosed within less than one month of symptom onset, versus forty percent of acute Lyme disease patients, used as a proxy for diagnostic delay. The Long COVID paper states explicitly that diagnostic delay to initial COVID nineteen diagnosis or long COVID recognition is not analyzed as a burden category, since the cohort's focus is on symptom persistence after an already-confirmed diagnosis. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On how official or claims-based data counts the condition. The Lyme disease paper notes that a cited claims-based study found adding an antibiotic-prescription criterion reduced the eligible sample by eighty-five percent, and that about fifty percent of Lyme disease patients are treated by clinicians not covered by insurance and so would not appear in insurance or electronic health records databases, meaning claims-based sources produce lower or different counts in the setting studied. The Long COVID paper does not discuss insurance claims, administrative data, or undercounting by official data sources anywhere in the document. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On access barriers (insurance coverage). The Lyme disease paper reports that about fifty percent of Lyme disease patients are treated by clinicians whose services are not covered by insurance, a figure the authors cite from their own earlier survey. The Long COVID paper states that insurance coverage and access barriers are not discussed anywhere in the document. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On out-of-pocket and direct medical cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper states plainly that it reports no direct medical costs, non-medical costs, or dollar-denominated indirect costs anywhere. The Long COVID paper likewise states that direct medical costs, non-medical costs, and monetary lost income are not measured or reported anywhere in the study. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On demographic subgroup breakdown of burden findings. The Lyme disease paper reports its burden measures (misdiagnosis, disability, health status) pooled by persistent Lyme disease-versus-acute Lyme disease disease stage only, with no breakdown by sex, age, race or ethnicity, or other demographic subgroup despite collecting sex and region data. The Long COVID paper reports its presenteeism, absenteeism, and economic-condition findings pooled by symptom trajectory, symptom cluster, and symptom count only, with no breakdown by sex or age despite collecting both in Table one. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers.

What the two papers share: Neither paper reports dollar-denominated costs of any kind - the burden evidence in both is expressed as percentages, scores, or proxy measures rather than money. Both papers pool their key burden findings without breaking them down by demographic subgroup, even though some demographic data were collected.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper provides dollar-denominated cost data, so no cross-condition cost comparison is possible from these two papers alone. Neither paper breaks down its burden findings by demographic subgroups such as sex, age, or race or ethnicity despite collecting some demographic data.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, three of them here. Lyme paper flags a large prior-misdiagnosis gap worth tracking elsewhere. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers add a measure of prior misdiagnosis or diagnostic delay to their thirty-six-month cohort follow-ups? Lyme paper measures work-disability status, not just productivity. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers report the share of their cohort classified as work-disabled, alongside their presenteeism scores? Long COVID paper scores productivity loss while still employed. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could Lyme disease researchers add a presenteeism-style productivity score for patients who are working but symptomatic?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an artificial intelligence model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used three model calls. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This analysis is based on a single Lyme disease paper and a single Long COVID paper, with no papers covering both conditions together; it is a small, non-random sample limited to whichever documents happened to be uploaded, not a controlled or representative comparison of the two conditions. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision.
