This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Chronic Lyme Disease Symptom Burden: Patient-Reported Utilization, Cost, and Employment Impact, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID Quality-of-Life Burden Simulator, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers - one on chronic Lyme disease symptom burden and one on Long COVID quality of life - and does not describe either condition in general. Both papers separately report a gap between patient-reported experience and official diagnosis or surveillance figures, and both report worse health-related quality of life for the affected group than for a comparison population, though each uses its own distinct outcome measure that cannot be converted into the other's units. The Lyme disease paper is the only one of the two with direct out-of-pocket cost percentages, employment-loss figures, and diagnostic-delay percentages; the Long COVID paper is the only one with standardized EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire or quality-adjusted life-month quality-of-life metrics and comparisons to other chronic diseases.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers only - one Lyme disease paper and one Long COVID paper - so it describes what these two specific papers report, not either condition in general. Both papers show a gap between patient-reported experience and official or administrative recognition: the Lyme disease paper contrasts a prior Centers for Disease Control and Prevention surveillance count of thirty thousand cases per year with a twenty thirteen Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimate of three hundred thousand diagnosed cases per year, while the Long COVID paper finds twenty-four point six percent of its cohort self-reported long COVID versus under ten percent with an electronic health records-recorded diagnosis code, a gap of eighteen point six percentage points. Only the Lyme disease paper provides direct out-of-pocket cost figures and employment-loss figures; the Long COVID paper reports no monetary figures at all and measures burden instead through standardized quality-of-life scores (EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire utility, QALMs).

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has eight dimensions; these are the first six. On out-of-pocket cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper reports that the share of chronic Lyme disease respondents with out-of-pocket costs exceeding two thousand dollars per year was forty-nine percentage points higher than the general-population comparator (twenty thirteen price year), and that forty-three percent of the sample reported costs exceeding five thousand dollars in the preceding year (twenty thirteen price year). The Long COVID paper reports no direct medical cost or out-of-pocket cost figures of its own; it states no currency or price-year figures are reported anywhere in the document. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On work and income loss. The Lyme disease paper reports an employment-rate gap of thirty-five point one percentage points (ages twenty-five to fifty-four) between chronic Lyme disease respondents and the general population, and that forty-two percent of respondents stopped working as a result of Lyme disease. The Long COVID paper does not measure employment or income loss itself; it only notes, by citing a separate external paper, that long COVID may affect labour output, without presenting this as its own measured finding. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On diagnostic delay. The Lyme disease paper reports that sixty-one point seven percent of its sample was not diagnosed within two years of symptom onset. The Long COVID paper reports no diagnostic-delay data; it states it has no information on the date of the COVID nineteen episode relative to symptom onset, which prevents modeling time-to-diagnosis. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On quality of life and symptom burden. The Lyme disease paper reports twenty point one poor physical health days per thirty and fifteen point five poor mental health days per thirty among chronic Lyme disease respondents, compared with three point seven and three point five days respectively in its general-population comparator. The Long COVID paper reports a mean EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire utility score of zero point five for self-reported long COVID versus zero point seven for non-long COVID respondents, and a quality-adjusted life-month loss difference of zero point four QALMs attributable to self-reported long COVID. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On how each condition's official or administrative data compares to patient-reported figures. The Lyme disease paper cites a prior Centers for Disease Control and Prevention surveillance count of thirty thousand cases per year alongside a twenty thirteen Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimate of three hundred thousand diagnosed cases per year, stating only that the surveillance system does not reflect the full spectrum of disease seen clinically, without saying one number is the true count. The Long COVID paper reports that twenty-four point six percent of its cohort self-reported long COVID while fewer than ten percent (six percent in its table) had an electronic health records-recorded diagnosis code, an eighteen point six percentage-point gap, showing that diagnosis-code-based counts produce lower or different counts in the setting studied. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On access barriers and insurance coverage. The Lyme disease paper describes, without quantifying, that many insurers have adopted restrictive Lyme disease treatment guidelines and may decline to cover extended antibiotic therapy, pushing patients toward out-of-network, higher out-of-pocket care. The Long COVID paper states that insurance and access barriers are not applicable or discussed in its United Kingdom National Health Service context and are not addressed in the document. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers.

What the two papers share: Both papers separately report a gap between how patients or respondents describe their own condition and what official diagnosis codes or surveillance counts capture, without either paper claiming to know the true underlying number. Both papers report markedly worse health-related quality of life for the affected group relative to a comparison population, each using its own distinct outcome measure.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper in this comparison provides a total monetary cost of illness (medical expenditure or productivity loss in dollar terms); both state this calculation was outside their scope or not supplied. Neither paper collected caregiver time or informal-care cost data. Neither paper provides data allowing extrapolation of its findings beyond its own sample's time window (a single preceding-year snapshot for the Lyme disease paper; roughly zero to three months of follow-up for the Long COVID paper) to a lifetime or annualized burden estimate.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, four of them here. Many Lyme patients wait years for diagnosis; does Long COVID? The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers measure the typical time between symptom onset and formal diagnosis, the way the Lyme disease paper did? Lyme disease paper reports a large out-of-pocket cost gap. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers collect comparable out-of-pocket spending data to see whether a similar cost gap exists? Lyme disease paper reports a large employment rate gap. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers compare employment rates between people with and without long COVID in a similarly aged cohort? Long COVID paper sizes quality-of-life loss with standard scales. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could Lyme disease researchers administer EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire or quality-adjusted life-month-based surveys to allow direct quality-of-life comparisons across conditions?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an A I model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used three model calls at a total cost of about thirty-four cents. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This comparison is based on a small, non-random sample of available papers: exactly one Lyme disease paper and one Long COVID paper, with no cross-condition or unconfirmed papers contributing. It is not a controlled study; the two papers use different populations, countries, time windows, and outcome measures, so similarities and differences described here reflect what these specific papers happen to report rather than a general comparison of the two conditions. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.
