This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Lyme Disease Diagnosis Frequency: Claims-Based Estimate versus Surveillance Counts.

This Centers for Disease Control and Prevention dispatch estimates how many people in the United States were actually diagnosed and treated for Lyme disease each year from twenty ten to twenty eighteen, using commercial insurance claims data rather than relying only on cases reported to public health surveillance.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulator models only the case-frequency correction pipeline reported in a cited study (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention dispatch, using twenty ten to twenty eighteen MarketScan commercial insurance claims data): a standardized, age-corrected count of people diagnosed and treated for Lyme disease, adjusted by a correction factor for missing diagnosis codes, compared against the much smaller national surveillance case count.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: This Centers for Disease Control and Prevention analysis of insurance claims suggests that far more people are diagnosed and treated for Lyme disease each year than appear in official health department counts.

For care delivery teams: This study indicates that the real clinical volume of Lyme disease diagnosis and treatment may be substantially larger than what state surveillance systems capture, partly because medical records often omit the Lyme-specific billing code even when a patient met surveillance criteria, and partly because presumptive (empiric) treatment in practice leads to some unquantified degree of overdiagnosis and overtreatment.

For payers and policy audiences: The study estimates that approximately four hundred seventy-six thousand people per year (twenty ten to twenty eighteen, ninety-five percent credible interval four hundred five thousand-five hundred forty-seven thousand) were diagnosed and treated for Lyme disease nationally based on standardized, corrected commercial claims data, compared with about thirty-five thousand cases per year captured through national passive surveillance - roughly a fourteen-fold difference at the document's own default figures.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Lower undercoding correction: Undercoding correction factor from two point three multiplier to one point five multiplier. Then Estimated annual diagnosed-and-treated Lyme cases moves from four hundred seventy-seven thousand six hundred fifty patients per year to three hundred seven thousand five hundred patients per year; and Claims-based estimate minus surveillance-reported cases moves from four hundred forty-two thousand six hundred fifty patients per year to two hundred seventy-two thousand five hundred patients per year. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred thirty-six units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from five from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance, three from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, two from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tick surveillance. Then generative artificial intelligence, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code. Computer code, not the artificial intelligence, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. Two quantities from the document were read for checking; one was compared with federal data and one could not be compared. Cases reported annually through national Lyme disease surveillance (comparison baseline) (twenty ten to twenty eighteen average, the United States): the document reports thirty-five thousand; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's reported cases averaged thirty-four thousand nine hundred seventy-three (confirmed and probable together) (yearly totals ranged thirty thousand one hundred fifty-eight to forty-two thousand seven hundred forty-three), so the document's figure is within ten percent of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention confirmed-plus-probable total (+zero point one percent versus the total). Estimated annual frequency of clinician-diagnosed and treated Lyme disease (twenty ten to twenty eighteen average, the United States): the document's estimate of four hundred seventy-six thousand is thirteen point six times the thirty-four thousand nine hundred seventy-three cases Centers for Disease Control and Prevention received as reported (yearly totals ranged thirty thousand one hundred fifty-eight to forty-two thousand seven hundred forty-three). Reported cases are a passive-surveillance subset, so estimates from claims or models are usually higher; this shows the size of the gap, not which figure is right. Federal source: public health surveillance (surveillance report), two thousand eight to twenty twenty-one; computed in code from the federal file; no artificial intelligence model. The model cited one of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Estimated annual diagnosed-and-treated Lyme cases is four hundred seventy-seven thousand six hundred fifty patients per year; Claims-based estimate minus surveillance-reported cases is four hundred forty-two thousand six hundred fifty patients per year; Low-incidence-state diagnosis share difference is fourteen percentage points.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Lower undercoding correction sets Undercoding correction factor to one point five multiplier, so Estimated annual diagnosed-and-treated Lyme cases becomes three hundred seven thousand five hundred patients per year; Claims-based estimate minus surveillance-reported cases becomes two hundred seventy-two thousand five hundred patients per year. Higher undercoding correction sets Undercoding correction factor to three point five multiplier, so Estimated annual diagnosed-and-treated Lyme cases becomes seven hundred seventeen thousand five hundred patients per year; Claims-based estimate minus surveillance-reported cases becomes six hundred eighty-two thousand five hundred patients per year. Surveillance at reported low end sets Surveillance-reported Lyme cases per year to thirty thousand cases per year, so Claims-based estimate minus surveillance-reported cases becomes four hundred forty-seven thousand six hundred fifty patients per year. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Estimated annual diagnosed-and-treated Lyme cases, those draws run from two hundred twenty-eight thousand three hundred fifty-three patients per year to eight hundred eighty-three thousand four hundred ninety-four patients per year, with a mean of five hundred nine thousand one hundred thirteen patients per year. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: undercounted by official surveillance. The other fifteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an artificial intelligence model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
