This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Lyme Antibiotic Retreatment Trials: Statistical Power and Adverse-Event Reanalysis, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID Employment and Income Disruption: Illustrative Scenario Tool, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers: one Lyme disease paper that statistically reanalyzes four antibiotic retreatment trials and reports trial-level adverse-event rates, and one Long COVID paper that qualitatively interviews twenty-one patients about employment and income effects and offers illustrative income-loss scenarios. The two papers do not measure the same things: the Lyme disease paper's only quantified figures are adverse-event rates from clinical trials, while the Long COVID paper's only quantified figures are illustrative annual income-loss amounts and a cited external lost-wages estimate. Both papers independently note, in their own words, that the official processes they describe (treatment guidelines, diagnostic practice for accommodations) rest on evidence each paper itself calls insufficient.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers: one Lyme disease paper focused on antibiotic retreatment trial safety and one Long COVID paper focused on employment and income effects, so findings describe only these two papers, not the conditions in general. The Lyme disease paper reports trial-level adverse-event rates (Krupp ten point nine percent, Fallon eighteen point nine percent) but no dollar costs anywhere, while the Long COVID paper reports illustrative annual income-loss figures (nine thousand nine hundred dollars and twelve thousand three hundred seventy-five dollars twenty twenty-two) but no measured adverse-event data. Both papers flag a systemic gap: the Lyme disease paper says treatment guidelines rest on trials it argues were underpowered, and the Long COVID paper says there is a lack of established diagnostic practice that makes workplace accommodation requests harder to support.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has eight dimensions; these are the first six. On out-of-pocket or direct cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper reports no dollar costs at all for medical care, non-medical costs, or retreatment; it explicitly states no price figures are given anywhere in the document. The Long COVID paper does not quantify direct medical or non-medical costs for its own interview sample; it reports participants describing insurance disruption and 'accumulating debt' only qualitatively, with no amount given. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On work and income loss. The Lyme disease paper contains no indirect cost data such as lost income, productivity loss, or caregiving time for patients or caregivers. The Long COVID paper gives illustrative annual income-loss figures of nine thousand nine hundred dollars (twenty twenty-two) if unable to work and twelve thousand three hundred seventy-five dollars (twenty twenty-two) under a reduced-schedule scenario, and separately cites a one hundred seventy billion dollars (twenty twenty-two) national lost-wages estimate from another source. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On diagnostic delay or diagnostic barriers. The Lyme disease paper notes persistent illness after a standard antibiotic course is 'not uncommon, particularly when treatment is delayed' and that no sensitive lab test for active infection is clinically available, but gives no percentage or quantified delay. The Long COVID paper notes a 'lack of established practices to diagnose Long COVID,' making it harder to support accommodation requests without objective diagnostic data, described qualitatively with no delay figure. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On access barriers to treatment or accommodations. The Lyme disease paper describes ongoing medical-community disagreement over whether persistent infection exists and whether retreatment helps, implying fragmented care, but does not quantify care-seeking patterns or access barriers. The Long COVID paper describes access barriers to coping strategies, employer disability benefits, and workplace accommodations, including employer resistance, but only qualitatively with no rates. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On adverse events or treatment-related harm. The Lyme disease paper reports trial-specific adverse-event rates of ten point nine percent (Krupp) and eighteen point nine percent (Fallon), an eight percentage-point difference between the two trials. The Long COVID paper contains no adverse-event or treatment-harm data; its scope is employment and income effects. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On systemic gaps in official guidance or diagnostic practice. The Lyme disease paper states that until a sensitive lab test for active infection exists, treatment guidelines rest on four trials the paper itself argues were underpowered or analyzed with statistically inefficient methods. The Long COVID paper states there is a lack of established practices to diagnose Long COVID, forcing reliance on documented functional limitations rather than diagnostic testing for accommodation requests. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers.

What the two papers share: Both papers describe a gap between the evidence actually available and the official processes (treatment guidelines in one case, diagnostic practice for accommodations in the other) that depend on it. Neither paper's own data quantifies direct dollar costs, even though each touches on burdens (treatment-related harm in one, income and insurance disruption in the other) that would normally carry a cost.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper provides direct medical or non-medical dollar cost figures for its own study population. Neither paper offers a quantified diagnostic-delay figure, even though both describe diagnostic uncertainty or lack of established diagnostic practice in words.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, two of them here. Ask whether a treatment itself carries a measurable side-effect rate. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers quantify an adverse-event rate for treatments used in their trials, the way the Lyme disease paper did for retreatment? Ask whether illness-related income loss can be put in dollar terms. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could Lyme disease researchers construct similar illustrative income-loss scenarios for patients affected by prolonged illness or retreatment?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an A I model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used three model calls at a total cost of about twenty-nine cents. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This is a small, non-random comparison based on whichever documents happened to be uploaded: one paper for Lyme disease and one paper for Long COVID, with no paper covering both conditions. It is not a controlled study, and the two papers used different methods (a trial biostatistical reanalysis versus a qualitative interview study), different populations, and different time windows, so no causal or population-level conclusions should be drawn from this comparison. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.
