This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, MyLymeData Chartbook: Diagnosis Timing, Misdiagnosis, Access Barriers, and Utilization in Persistent Lyme Disease, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID and Financial Hardship Risk, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers - the Lyme disease paper (one persistent Lyme disease patient-registry chartbook) and the Long COVID paper (one Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System-based financial-hardship study) - and does not describe either condition in general. The Lyme disease paper is rich in diagnostic-delay, access-barrier, and healthcare-utilization detail but offers only one externally cited aggregate dollar figure rather than its own patient-level cost data. The Long COVID paper is rich in income- and education-stratified financial-hardship probabilities, with employment loss identified as a partial contributing channel, but contains no dollar costs, diagnostic-delay data, or access-barrier data at all.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers - one on Lyme disease and one on Long COVID - and does not describe either condition in general. The Lyme disease paper contains extensive diagnostic-delay, access-barrier, and healthcare-utilization figures that the Long COVID paper does not address at all, while the Long COVID paper contains income- and education-stratified financial-hardship probabilities that the Lyme disease paper does not report. Neither paper supplies a direct patient out-of-pocket dollar figure: the Lyme disease paper cites one external, claims-based one billion dollars per year estimate, and the Long COVID paper reports only percentage-point changes in the probability of a hardship event, with no dollar amounts at all.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has eight dimensions; these are the first six. On direct dollar-cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper cites an external, claims-based estimate of one billion dollars per year in United States healthcare costs tied to retreatment of diagnosed Lyme patients, but this is not the paper's own patient-level out-of-pocket figure and no price year is given. The Long COVID paper reports no dollar-denominated cost figure of any kind; all of its burden measures are percentage-point changes in the probability of a hardship event (food insecurity, inability to pay bills, threatened loss of utility service), not expenditures. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On diagnostic delay. The Lyme disease paper reports that seventy-seven percent of persistent Lyme disease patients were not diagnosed until the late or chronic stage (over six months), a thirty-two-percentage-point gap in prior misdiagnosis between persistent and acute patients, and that eighty-six percent of surveyed clinicians cite inadequate physician education as a reason for delayed diagnosis. The Long COVID paper does not tag or report any diagnostic-delay figures. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On work and income loss. The Lyme disease paper reports that, among persistent Lyme disease patients with a work-schedule impact, sixty-two percent reduced work hours and thirty percent quit work altogether, and that thirty-five percent of persistent Lyme disease patients report disability as their employment status. The Long COVID paper finds that lost or reduced work hours account for a zero point seven percentage-point portion of the bill-payment-hardship increase among income groups, and more broadly that six percent-twenty percent of the long-COVID-to-financial-hardship association is mediated by lost employment or reduced work hours. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On access barriers to care. The Lyme disease paper reports that fifty percent of persistent Lyme disease patients' physicians do not accept insurance, and that roughly seventy-five percent of surveyed Lyme-treating clinicians do not participate in insurance networks. The Long COVID paper does not tag or report an access-barriers category; it measures financial hardship events rather than provider-access or insurance-acceptance barriers. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On healthcare utilization. The Lyme disease paper reports a ratio of seventeen point eight for persistent Lyme disease patients' annual doctor visits compared to the general-population comparator, and separately states nineteen point four annual doctor visits versus one point one for the general population, and three point seven annual emergency room visits versus zero point four. The Long COVID paper does not report healthcare utilization counts such as doctor or emergency room visits; it measures only the probability of financial-hardship events. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On how cited or modeled figures may undercount the burden. The Lyme disease paper notes that its cited one billion dollars per year estimate is drawn from insurance-claims records, and by the paper's own account roughly fifty percent of persistent Lyme disease patients' physicians do not accept insurance, so this estimate may produce lower or different counts in the setting studied. The Long COVID paper does not include an undercounting-related limitation in its caveats, and it does not report any cost estimate that could be checked against an alternative data source. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it.

What the two papers share: Neither paper provides a patient-level, condition-specific dollar figure for out-of-pocket spending; where a dollar figure exists at all (Lyme disease), it comes from an external claims-based source rather than the paper's own registry data, and the Long COVID paper has no dollar figure whatsoever. Both papers separately identify lost or reduced work or employment as a contributing channel to overall burden, even though they quantify it with different metrics.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper provides a direct, patient-level, condition-specific dollar figure for out-of-pocket medical spending tied to its own underlying data source. Neither paper's reported figures are broken down by race or ethnicity, disability status, sexual orientation, gender identity, veteran status, or marital or familial status, even where some of these variables appear elsewhere in the underlying data (for example, as regression covariates in the Long COVID paper).

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, three of them here. Half of persistent Lyme patients face a doctor-insurance mismatch. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers measure what share of patients report their treating clinician does not accept their insurance? Persistent-disease patients report far more pre-diagnosis misdiagnosis. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID studies compare misdiagnosis rates between patients with prolonged versus brief symptom courses? A single cited billion-dollar figure stands in for real cost data. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers develop or cite a comparable aggregate retreatment- or care-cost estimate, and would it rely on original patient data or an external source like the Lyme figure does?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an A I model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used three model calls at a total cost of about thirty-two cents. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This analysis rests on a small, non-random sample: as given in the library counts, there is one paper in the Lyme disease group and one paper in the Long COVID group, with zero papers in the cross or other groups, so this is not a controlled study and cannot be generalized beyond these two specific papers and the documents each was built from. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.
