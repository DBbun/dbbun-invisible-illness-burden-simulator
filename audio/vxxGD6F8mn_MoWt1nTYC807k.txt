This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Long COVID Quality-of-Life Burden Simulator (OpenPROMPT United Kingdom Cohort).

This is a United Kingdom cohort study (OpenPROMPT, published in The Lancet Regional Health - Europe, twenty twenty-four) that linked smartphone-collected patient-reported outcome surveys to National Health Service electronic health records for six thousand seventy adults to measure how long COVID affects health-related quality of life using the EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire questionnaire.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulation reproduces health-related quality-of-life (health-related quality of life) and quality-adjusted life-month findings from a United Kingdom cohort study (OpenPROMPT, sample size six thousand seventy adults, surveys linked to National Health Service records, late twenty twenty-two-mid twenty twenty-three, zero-three month follow-up) comparing people who self-reported long COVID to those who did not.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: In this United Kingdom study, people who reported long COVID had notably worse day-to-day health status than people who did not, using a standard zero-one health-status survey (EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire) and a combined measure of health quality over time (quality-adjusted life-months, or QALMs - a score that combines how good your health is with how long you experienced that level).

For care delivery teams: This cohort found a large, statistically robust association between self-reported long COVID and health-related quality of life loss (odds ratio four point seven), which shrank markedly (to one point five, no longer statistically significant) once fatigue and breathlessness patient-reported outcome scores were added to the model - suggesting fatigue and breathlessness account for much of the quality-of-life signal, though breathlessness severity grades themselves were not significant independent predictors of whether a patient reported any loss at all.

For payers and policy audiences: This study provides United Kingdom cohort-level evidence that self-reported long COVID is associated with substantially reduced health-related quality of life (odds ratio four point seven, unadjusted) and measurable quality-adjusted life-month loss, with quality-of-life scores the authors describe as comparable to or worse than published scores for heart failure, multiple sclerosis, and end-stage renal disease (cited from other sources, not measured here).

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Widest plausible quality-adjusted life-month gap: Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss, self-reported long COVID from zero point eight QALMs (quality-adjusted life-months lost) to one QALMs (quality-adjusted life-months lost) and Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss, non-long COVID from zero point four QALMs (quality-adjusted life-months lost) to zero point two QALMs (quality-adjusted life-months lost). Then Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss difference, long COVID minus non-long COVID moves from zero point four QALMs lost to zero point eight QALMs lost. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, four hundred thirty-eight units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from four from Household Pulse Survey Long COVID data from the United States Census Bureau, three from Bureau of Economic Analysis regional economics data, two from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Long COVID aggregate data for twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about seventy-five cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. No federal check applied to this document. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss difference, long COVID minus non-long COVID is zero point four QALMs lost; EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire utility score, long COVID minus non-long COVID is minus zero point two utility points (zero-one scale); Self-reported long COVID percent minus electronic health records-diagnosis-coded percent is eighteen point six percentage points; Unadjusted minus patient-reported outcome measures-adjusted odds ratio for health-related quality of life loss is three point two odds ratio points (difference in odds ratios).

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Widest plausible quality-adjusted life-month gap sets Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss, self-reported long COVID to one QALMs (quality-adjusted life-months lost) and Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss, non-long COVID to zero point two QALMs (quality-adjusted life-months lost), so Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss difference, long COVID minus non-long COVID becomes zero point eight QALMs lost. Narrowest plausible quality-adjusted life-month gap sets Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss, self-reported long COVID to zero point five QALMs (quality-adjusted life-months lost) and Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss, non-long COVID to zero point five QALMs (quality-adjusted life-months lost), so Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss difference, long COVID minus non-long COVID becomes zero QALMs lost. Widest assumed utility gap sets Mean EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire utility score, self-reported long COVID to zero point three utility points (zero-one scale, one = full health) and Mean EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire utility score, non-long COVID to zero point nine utility points (zero-one scale, one = full health), so EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire utility score, long COVID minus non-long COVID becomes minus zero point six utility points (zero-one scale). These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Complete-case quality-adjusted life-month loss difference, long COVID minus non-long COVID, those draws run from zero QALMs lost to zero point eight QALMs lost, with a mean of zero point four QALMs lost. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for two of them: overall quality of life, undercounted by official surveillance. The other fourteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and direct costs of care. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
