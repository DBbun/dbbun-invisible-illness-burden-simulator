This is an educational what-if tool, not medical advice.

This audio overview explains the whole D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator: what it is, every feature you will find in it, and why it could matter for patients, for care teams, and for the people who set policy. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts, as an entry in the Cost of Illness track of the T O P x H H S Tech Sprint for A I and Invisible Illness, run by the United States Department of Health and Human Services. It is an independent entry. It is not endorsed by the Department of Health and Human Services or the National Institutes of Health.

Start with the problem. When researchers publish what an illness costs, the result is usually a static document. A patient cannot ask what happens if their own costs are thirty percent higher. An analyst cannot easily tell one study's figure from a population estimate. And the burdens a paper never measured are invisible, because nobody writes a sentence about what is missing.

The simulator turns a published paper, or up to five related documents, into a working what-if model. It does that for Lyme disease and Long COVID, the two conditions it was built and checked for. In this public demonstration you can open twenty-five finished simulators, built from twenty-three research papers and two reports. Twelve are about Lyme disease and thirteen are about Long COVID. Each has a permanent number, from one to twenty-five, so people can refer to them. The Simulation library button at the top lists them, with a filter for each condition, a map of which burdens each one measures, and a button to listen to an audio overview of each simulator.

Let us begin with the value for the three kinds of users. Every simulator is written for all three at once, and one switch at the top of the results page changes the wording. The numbers never change.

For patients and advocates, the value is plain language and per-person figures: a way to see what a study actually measured, what it only assumed, and what it never looked at, and to turn that into better questions for a clinician or an advocacy group.

For care delivery teams, the value is workflow and resourcing questions. For example, how a study's findings on delayed diagnosis compare with a clinic's own referral timelines.

For payers and policy teams, the value is budget impact and population totals, and a clear separation between a study's cohort figure and a national estimate, before anyone puts a number in a budget.

Now, how does it work? You upload a document in the full tool. Its text, table captions and figures are extracted on the local machine. A small local classifier then decides whether the paper is about Lyme disease, Long COVID, or both. That is classical machine learning, using keyword matching and a text-similarity method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. It is not a large language model, and it makes no model call.

That decision determines which federal evidence is eligible. The simulator draws on a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units. A unit is one file from a federal dataset, such as a data table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. The units come from thirty federal dataset families across twelve agencies, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, the Census Bureau, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Bureau of Economic Analysis, and the Health Resources and Services Administration. A retrieval step scores the eligible units against your document and offers the ten most relevant to the A I model as background.

Then generative A I does its part. Claude Sonnet is called four times. First it extracts the paper's findings. Second it drafts the interactive simulator and its documentation. Third it writes named what-if scenarios and forecasts. Fourth it writes a Python version of the model that you can download. Each run takes about four to nine minutes and costs well under one dollar in model fees. The model reads the paper and writes wording. It does not do the arithmetic, and it does not compare anything with federal data. Ordinary computer code does that, which means anyone can inspect it.

Every number on the screen carries a label. Observed means the paper reported or measured it, with the page. Assumed means it was filled in because the paper did not report it, with the range stated. Projected means it was computed forward from a rate, as a straight line, not as a prediction.

Next, the result screen. It has seven tabs.

Overview opens with the takeaway: an at-a-glance strip, a plain-language summary, which assumptions move the results most, and a map of sixteen kinds of burden showing which ones this paper measured. Across all twenty-five simulators, each has results tagged to an average of one point four of the sixteen burdens, and none to more than three. Six burdens have no tagged result in any simulator, for either condition: caregiving, cognitive impact, psychological burden, care continuity, parent-to-child transmission, and long-term opportunity costs. The simulator shows that gap instead of hiding it.

Operation Flow shows what happened to your document, step by step, with that simulator's own numbers, so nothing about how a result was produced is hidden.

Explore is the interactive core. Pick a ready-made named scenario, or move the sliders yourself. Every result recalculates instantly in your browser, by ordinary arithmetic. A panel lists which inputs you changed and which results they feed.

Forecast looks ahead where a paper reports a per-period rate. The darker band covers the middle eighty percent of five hundred random draws inside each assumption's range. It is labeled illustrative and is not a confidence interval.

Sensitivity moves one input at a time and ranks which assumptions matter most.

Evidence and Sources is the audit. It shows which federal data were offered to the model, and the computed checks. Code restates dollar figures in current prices, compares Lyme case counts with national surveillance, and compares Long COVID prevalence with a federal survey. It also shows a table of who bears the cost, with a label when a paper does not say. Federal data gives context and checks. It never supplies a simulator's numbers.

Export lets you take it with you: a formatted documentation P D F, a nine-sheet Excel workbook, C S V files that R can read, including the five hundred random draws, the model specification, the Python source of the same model, which is generated by the A I model and has not been run against the simulator's own results, and a prototype export in the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources standard.

Beyond single simulators, there are thirteen saved cross-pollination analyses. Each places one Lyme disease paper beside one Long COVID paper and asks what each field could learn from the other. One real finding shows why. A diagnostic-delay result appears in six of the twelve Lyme disease simulators and in none of the thirteen Long COVID simulators. The comparison turns that mismatch into a question for Long COVID researchers: could the time to diagnosis be measured the way the Lyme papers did it? The numbers in each comparison are copied from the simulators, and the wording is checked in code.

Every simulator and every comparison also has a spoken overview, with a transcript and an M P three download. The voice is synthetic, made with the open Kokoro text-to-speech model, and each recording says so. They were added after an advocate who cannot read long text asked for audio.

For interoperability, each model exports as Observation resources in the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources standard, version four, with provenance back to the source paper. All twenty-six files, from twenty-five simulators and one reuse example, pass the official validator with no errors, with terminology checks switched off. It is a prototype. It contains no patient and is not clinical data. A Blue Button 2.0 demonstration signs in against the federal sandbox with synthetic test beneficiaries and shows yearly claim totals beside a simulator. It has no access to real Medicare data. The full tool also has a documented programming interface, so another system can submit papers or call a finished simulator. And an Administrator tab in the full tool lets the owner manage the federal evidence library. It has its own audio overview.

The tool also carries guardrails. It flags figures that come from insurance claims or diagnosis codes, because those codes miss many real cases of Lyme disease and Long COVID. It discloses findings that differ by sex or gender instead of pooling them. It flags when a paper itself reports a funding gap or a gap in the care system. And it never recommends or ranks treatments.

The design came from people. During the ten-week sprint, the developer held eight interviews, with six patient advocates, one Long COVID patient and one government deputy director, and posted questions on a public Lyme discussion board, where fifteen of the seventeen people asked replied. Federal reviewers asked for price-year labels and payer labels, now shown wherever a paper states them. A reply from a person with Lyme about the work of coordinating fragmented care confirmed a burden category for uncoordinated care. A state policy official asked for a structured spreadsheet for budget forecasting, which became the Excel export. Each change is listed in a one-page feedback log published with this demonstration.

Now, why could this matter? The sprint judges every entry on four criteria, so here they are, one by one, with the potential and the limits stated plainly.

The first is results for the American people. The potential is to make costs that are invisible visible: an advocate can see which burden a paper never measured, a care team can see which assumption it must not treat as a patient fact, and an analyst can tell a study's figure from a population estimate. Lived experience is built into the burden categories, the audio, the labels and the wording. The limit: it is not a diagnostic tool, it gives no medical advice, patients are not expected to be its main direct users, and no outside person has yet completed a documented task in it.

The second is data, technology and evidence-driven innovation. The potential is connecting fragmented sources: a paper, federal surveillance, surveys and price indexes, tied together by classical machine learning, generative A I used only for reading and wording, and code for every calculation and check. The limit: federal data checks and contextualizes. Retrieval is keyword-based, only three kinds of figures are cross-checked, and nothing has been clinically validated.

The third is execution, efficiency and delivery. The public demonstration works today: anyone can open all twenty-five simulators, change assumptions, read thirteen comparisons and download bundles. Generating a new simulator costs well under a dollar, and the automated test suite has over two hundred tests. The limit: generating a simulator from a new document runs on the owner's computer. There is no hosted self-service upload yet.

The fourth is scalability, reuse and societal impact. The potential is that the extraction, the labels, the arithmetic, the programming interface and the export are not written for one disease. What is specific to Lyme disease and Long COVID is the curated federal evidence, the instructions and the burden categories. Two papers on another condition were run through the same pipeline as a labeled demonstration. The policy case is the evidence gap itself: six burdens with no tagged result in this library, claims data that miss many cases, and the national health interview survey adult codebooks the developer reviewed for twenty twenty-two to twenty twenty-five, which contain no Lyme question. The limit: only Lyme disease and Long COVID have curated federal evidence, and the standards named for the United States Core Data for Interoperability Plus and the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement are not implemented.

So the concept could be transformative. In principle, the same approach can be applied to any published paper on these conditions, turning a static report into an adjustable model that is checked against federal data and states its own gaps. That is a potential, not a result. It could be extended to other diseases, and the vision is wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries, so that readers can explore the numbers in a paper instead of only reading them. That is a vision for the future, not something tested.

Once more: this is an educational what-if tool, not medical advice. Thank you for listening.
