This audio overview explains the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator. It was developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The example here is the simulator titled: Lyme Antibiotic Retreatment Trials: Statistical Power and Adverse-Event Reanalysis.

This is a peer-reviewed biostatistical reanalysis of the four National Institutes of Health-funded randomized, placebo-controlled trials that current (as of the paper's twenty twelve publication) Lyme disease guidelines relied on to recommend against antibiotic retreatment for patients with persistent symptoms after a standard course of treatment.

What it covers, and what it does not: This simulation reproduces only the statistical significance findings and trial-specific adverse-event counts reported in a biostatistical reanalysis of four National Institutes of Health-funded randomized, placebo-controlled Lyme disease antibiotic-retreatment trials.

First, what is the value? Every simulator is written for three kinds of users at once, and the results page can be switched between the three views.

For patients and advocates: Using the document's own reported numbers, one trial (Fallon) tested whether retreatment improved cognitive function after twelve weeks; its result (a p-value of zero point one) narrowly missed the paper's own cutoff for calling a result statistically significant (meaning unlikely to be due to chance) of p<zero point one, and the improvement was not sustained by twenty-four weeks.

For care delivery teams: This reanalysis distinguishes outcome domains that are often discussed together as one 'retreatment benefit' question but had different evidence strength: the Krupp trial's fatigue outcome reached statistical significance and was sustained, while the Fallon trial's twelve-week cognitive-function outcome (p=zero point one against the paper's own p<zero point one threshold) did not reach significance and was not sustained at twenty-four weeks, though secondary Fallon analyses found improvement in fatigue, pain, and physical functioning among patients with worse baseline severity.

For payers and policy audiences: The reanalysis argues that the Klempner trials and one Fallon outcome were designed around target treatment effects much larger than published minimum clinically important differences, making their 'no significant benefit' conclusions potentially a power or design artifact rather than proof of no effect.

For all three, the adjustable model lets them ask what changes if an assumption changes.

Why build it this way? A published paper gives you its numbers frozen in one year and one place. You cannot change an assumption, compare the figures with national data, or see what the paper never measured. In the simulator, the document becomes runnable, not only readable. For example, in the scenario named Krupp: serious events only: Krupp trial treatment-related adverse events from six to four and Krupp trial total randomized patients from fifty-five to fifty-five. Then Krupp trial adverse-event rate moves from ten point nine percent to seven point three percent; and Fallon minus Krupp adverse-event rate moves from eight percentage points to eleven point six percentage points. Every result updates at once, and nothing is re-typed or recalculated by hand.

How does it work? A small local text classifier decides which condition a document is about, using keyword matching and a method called term frequency, inverse document frequency. That is the machine learning step, and it is not a large language model. It selects eligible federal evidence from a pre-built library of about fourteen hundred and fifty-four units, where a unit is one federal data file, such as a table, a spreadsheet or a guideline document, pre-processed into a short text summary. For this document, three hundred ninety-seven units were eligible and ten were picked as short excerpts, from six from National Health Expenditure Accounts from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, two from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding guidelines, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tick surveillance, one from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Lyme disease surveillance. Then generative A I, Claude Sonnet, is called four times to extract the findings, draft the interactive simulator, write the scenarios, and write downloadable Python code, at a total cost here of about sixty-three cents. Computer code, not the A I, does the arithmetic and the federal checks. Every number is labeled observed, assumed or projected.

How were the federal resources used for this simulator? The numbers come from the document. Federal data decided which sources were eligible, was offered to the model as background, and feeds the computed checks. No federal check applied to this document. The model did not cite any of the ten retrieved units as evidence, and the simulator records that too.

Now the what-if scenarios. The simulator opens at the document's reported values. At those defaults, Fallon p-value minus significance threshold is zero; Krupp trial adverse-event rate is ten point nine percent; Fallon trial adverse-event rate is eighteen point nine percent; Fallon minus Krupp adverse-event rate is eight percentage points.

There are three named scenarios, one click each. Krupp: serious events only sets Krupp trial treatment-related adverse events to four and Krupp trial total randomized patients to fifty-five, so Krupp trial adverse-event rate becomes seven point three percent; Fallon minus Krupp adverse-event rate becomes eleven point six percentage points. Fallon: active arm only sets Fallon trial treatment-related adverse events to six and Fallon trial total randomized patients to twenty-three, so Fallon trial adverse-event rate becomes twenty-six point one percent; Fallon minus Krupp adverse-event rate becomes fifteen point two percentage points. Krupp: active arm only sets Krupp trial treatment-related adverse events to three and Krupp trial total randomized patients to twenty-eight, so Krupp trial adverse-event rate becomes ten point seven percent; Fallon minus Krupp adverse-event rate becomes eight point two percentage points. These scenario values are illustrative bounds chosen for the simulator, not findings from the source. They show how far a result moves, not what will happen.

This simulator has no forecast chart. It also uses Monte Carlo simulation. The Monte Carlo step draws five hundred random combinations of the assumptions, each within its own range. For Fallon p-value minus significance threshold, those draws run from minus zero point one to zero point two, with a mean of zero. That spread reflects only the assumed exploration ranges of the inputs. It is not a probability or a confidence interval. The draws and their summary are included in the downloads, as files named monte carlo sample and monte carlo summary.

What does this document not measure? The simulator sorts results into sixteen burden types. This document has results for one of them: harm from misdiagnosis-driven treatment. The other fifteen are empty here. They include work and income, caregiving and household labor, cognitive impact, psychological burden, and overall quality of life. An empty type is a gap in what this simulator covers, not proof that the burden is absent, and the document may discuss some of them without reporting a result.

The limits are these. Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed. Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code. Federal data shown to the model is not the same as federal data used. The burden categories are assigned by an A I model. And the simulator has not been validated as a forecast.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. This simulator is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.

You can take the results with you: a nine-sheet Excel workbook, comma-separated files and Python code, which statistical software and languages such as R can read, and there is a cross-pollination analysis that sets Lyme disease and Long COVID simulators side by side. In the full tool, an Application Programming Interface and an Administrator page for adding federal resources are also available.
