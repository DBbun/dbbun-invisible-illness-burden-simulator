This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, MyLymeData Chartbook: Diagnosis Timing, Misdiagnosis, Access Barriers, and Utilization in Persistent Lyme Disease, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID Phenotyping versus Diagnosis-Code Surveillance Gap Explorer, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers: the Lyme disease paper, built from a patient-registry chartbook, and the Long COVID paper, built from a peer-reviewed electronic-health-record phenotyping study. Both separately describe official counting methods producing lower or different counts than their own more detailed data sources, but the mechanisms differ: the Lyme disease paper points to insurance-claims exclusion of unbilled care, while the Long COVID paper points to diagnostic-code insensitivity. The Lyme disease paper offers detailed figures on diagnostic delay, access barriers, work loss, health status, and utilization that the Long COVID paper does not report, while the Long COVID paper offers detailed prevalence and chronicity figures with regional breakdowns that the Lyme disease paper does not attempt.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers: the Lyme disease paper and the Long COVID paper, and does not describe either condition in general. Both papers show that official counting methods (insurance claims coding for Lyme disease, diagnostic coding for Long COVID) produce lower or different counts in the setting studied compared with patient-registry or phenotyping-based identification. The Lyme disease paper reports detailed patient-reported figures on diagnostic delay, access barriers, work loss, and healthcare utilization, while the Long COVID paper reports detailed prevalence and chronicity figures but states it has no cost, income-loss, or utilization figures of its own.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has eight dimensions; these are the first six. On diagnostic delay and misdiagnosis before diagnosis. The Lyme disease paper reports a thirty-two percentage point gap in prior misdiagnosis between persistent Lyme disease patients and acute Lyme disease patients, and that patients diagnosed late (six or more months) show a fifty-seven percentage point higher persistent-disease rate than those diagnosed early. The Long COVID paper does not report a diagnostic-delay or misdiagnosis figure; its tagged categories are limited to surveillance undercount. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On how official data undercounts cases or costs. The Lyme disease paper notes that the a cited study twenty fifteen claims-based one billion dollars per year healthcare cost estimate is drawn from insurance claims records, which by the paper's own account likely excludes an unknown share of the roughly fifty percent of persistent Lyme disease patients whose providers do not accept insurance. The Long COVID paper reports that diagnosis-code-based surveillance (citing four point nine percent-nineteen percent sensitivity across health systems and under seven percent for broader code-based approaches) produces lower or different counts in the setting studied than its phenotyping method, which identified post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen in sixteen point three percent of COVID nineteen patients, a nine point three percentage point surveillance gap. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers. On access barriers to care. The Lyme disease paper reports that fifty percent of persistent Lyme disease patients say their physician does not accept insurance, and that about seventy-five percent of surveyed Lyme-treating clinicians do not participate in insurance networks. The Long COVID paper states it does not quantify access barriers directly; it only notes, as a stated limitation, that patients with limited or fragmented health care engagement may be underrepresented, which could underestimate post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen prevalence. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On direct dollar cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper cites an externally sourced estimate of one billion dollars per year in United States healthcare costs tied to Lyme disease retreatment (a cited study twenty fifteen, claims-based, payer: insurer or health system), though this is not the paper's own primary registry measurement. The Long COVID paper reports no dollar cost figures of its own; it only cites, without adopting, an external claim that post-acute sequelae of COVID nineteen patients generate approximately one point five times higher healthcare costs than matched controls, with no dollar amount or price year given. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On work and income loss. The Lyme disease paper reports that thirty-five percent of persistent Lyme disease patients have an employment status of disabled, sixty-two percent of those reporting a work-schedule impact reduced their hours, and thirty percent quit work altogether. The Long COVID paper does not report any work hours, income loss, or productivity figures; it lists this as an explicit evidence gap. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On patient-reported health status burden. The Lyme disease paper reports that seventy-two percent of persistent Lyme disease patients report fair or poor health status compared with thirteen percent of the general population (DeLong twenty nineteen estimate). The Long COVID paper does not report a patient-reported health-status or quality-of-life figure; its measures are prevalence and chronicity based on electronic health record coding. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it.

What the two papers share: Official counting systems (insurance claims for Lyme disease cost, diagnostic codes for Long COVID prevalence) produce lower or different counts in the setting studied than patient-level or phenotyping-based data. Both papers explicitly state they lack the cost and income-loss data needed to produce a total dollar burden estimate from their own primary data.

What neither paper provides: Neither the Lyme disease paper nor the Long COVID paper provides direct patient out-of-pocket cost or total dollar cost figures derived from its own primary data source. Neither paper breaks down its burden figures by age, race or ethnicity, disability status, or similar subgroup categories, beyond the disease-stage split (Lyme disease paper) or regional split (Long COVID paper) each already reports.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, four of them here. Many Lyme patients report doctors who don't accept their insurance. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID clinics and payers track how often patients encounter physicians who do not accept insurance? Persistent Lyme patients report far more pre-diagnosis misdiagnosis than acute patients. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers compare misdiagnosis rates between patients with shorter and longer symptom duration? Lyme disease paper cites a large annual retreatment healthcare cost. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers estimate the annual healthcare cost tied to prolonged or repeated treatment using claims data? Code-based counts may miss many actual Long COVID cases. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could Lyme disease researchers compare registry-based case identification to insurance-code surveillance to estimate a similar undercount gap?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an artificial intelligence model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used three model calls. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This comparison rests on a small, non-random sample: one Lyme disease paper and one Long COVID paper, with no cross-condition or unconfirmed papers available to add further evidence. It is not a controlled study, and agreement or disagreement between the two papers' findings does not validate either one; the papers were built from different source documents with different methods, populations, and time frames. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision.
