This audio overview explains one cross-pollination analysis from the D B bun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator, developed by Doctor Uri Kartoon, founder of D B bun L L C, in Cambridge, Massachusetts. A cross-pollination analysis sets one Lyme disease simulator beside one Long COVID simulator and asks what each condition's researchers could learn from the other. Each simulator is built from one published paper, and the analysis compares what the two papers report. It does not describe either condition in general.

The two simulators compared here are, first, Chronic Lyme Disease Diagnostic Delay and Care-Access Barriers, on the Lyme disease side, and second, Long COVID Incidence and Risk-Factor Explorer, on the Long COVID side.

The summary says: This comparison covers two papers, one built from a two thousand nine to twenty ten patient survey on chronic Lyme disease and one built from a twenty twenty-five electronic-health-record incidence study on Long COVID. The Lyme disease paper quantifies diagnostic delay, travel burden, hospital-access difficulty, and insurance or disability denial directly from patient-reported survey data, while the Long COVID paper quantifies excess incidence and clinical or demographic risk factors directly from electronic health records-based computable phenotypes. Neither paper reports direct dollar costs, out-of-pocket costs, or productivity-loss figures, and both flag unresolved issues with case definitions or guideline enforcement as a systemic limitation on their own findings.

The headline findings are these. This comparison covers two papers - one on Lyme disease and one on Long COVID - and does not describe either condition in general. The Lyme disease paper quantifies diagnostic delay, travel burden, hospital access difficulty, and insurance or disability denial, while the Long COVID paper quantifies excess incidence and risk factors but reports no comparable access-barrier or administrative-denial figures. Neither paper reports direct dollar cost, out-of-pocket cost, or productivity-loss figures, so no cost-based comparison can be made between the two conditions studied here.

Here is the side-by-side, dimension by dimension. The comparison has seven dimensions; these are the first six. On diagnostic delay. The Lyme disease paper reports that thirty-seven point five percent of surveyed patients saw ten or more physicians before being diagnosed, reflecting substantial diagnostic delay in its self-selected chronic-Lyme sample. The Long COVID paper does not report a diagnostic-delay figure; it only notes as an unquantified limitation that patients must seek care for a diagnosis to be documented in electronic health records. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On access barriers (travel, hospital access). The Lyme disease paper finds forty-nine point five percent of patients traveled more than fifty miles for treatment and eighty-one point seven percent of those who sought local hospital care reported difficulty obtaining it. The Long COVID paper does not quantify any access-barrier figure; it only mentions healthcare access variation by gender, race or ethnicity, and rurality as an unquantified limitation. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On insurance and disability denial (administrative barriers). The Lyme disease paper reports an insurance-denial-minus-disability-denial gap of one point three percentage points, with thirty-nine point nine percent of insurance applicants denied new coverage and thirty-eight point six percent of disability applicants denied benefits tied to non-conformity with Infectious Diseases Society of America treatment guidelines. The Long COVID paper does not tag an administrative-barrier category and reports no insurance-denial or disability-denial figures. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On out-of-pocket or direct cost evidence. The Lyme disease paper collected no dollar cost figures itself; it cites an outside two thousand six study only for context and explicitly says that study should not be pooled with its own survey percentages. The Long COVID paper reports no direct medical cost, out-of-pocket cost, or productivity-loss figures of any kind, stating this would require an entirely separate cost dataset. The comparison marks this as similar across the two papers. On work and income loss. The Lyme disease paper mentions job loss only by citing a separate, smaller nineteen ninety-three survey for context; its own two thousand nine to twenty ten survey did not measure job loss or income loss. The Long COVID paper does not mention work loss, income loss, or productivity loss anywhere in its findings. The comparison marks this as one-sided, meaning only one of the two papers measures it. On how official data may undercount each condition. The Lyme disease paper cites, as background context, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's acknowledged six- to twelve-fold underreporting multiplier applied to two thousand eight case counts (thirty-five thousand one hundred ninety-eight reported versus an estimated two hundred thousand-four hundred thousand cases), but this figure comes from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, not from the survey's own data collection. The Long COVID paper's own analysis is tagged surveillance_undercount: it computes excess incidence attributable to COVID nineteen of five point three percentage points in the NthreeC network's data and six point three percentage points in the PCORnet network's data, while its claims_undercounting limitation notes that electronic-health-record-based detection may produce lower or different counts in the setting studied. The comparison marks this as different between the two papers.

What the two papers share: Neither paper provides direct dollar cost figures for the condition it studies, relying instead on percentages, counts, or external citations for cost context. Both papers describe a systemic gap tied to inconsistent or contested standards for defining or classifying the condition, which the papers' authors say affects how patients are identified or treated.

What neither paper provides: Neither paper provides direct medical cost, out-of-pocket cost, or productivity-loss figures, so no dollar-based burden comparison is possible between the two conditions studied here. Neither paper's findings are broken down by age, gender, socioeconomic status, race or ethnicity, or rurality into disaggregated figures, though both note this as a limitation of their own data.

The comparison also lists questions worth asking, four of them here. Long COVID could track how many clinicians patients see before diagnosis. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers add a measure of the number of clinicians seen before diagnosis to their electronic health records-based studies? Travel distance and hospital access trouble are measurable, worth tracking. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers survey patients about travel distance and hospital-access difficulty alongside their incidence estimates? Insurance versus disability denial gaps are worth measuring too. The question worth asking, from the Lyme paper to Long COVID: Could Long COVID researchers compare insurance denial rates to disability-benefit denial rates for their patients? Electronic health records-based excess-incidence estimates could reveal hidden Lyme undercount. The question worth asking, from the Long COVID paper to Lyme disease: Could Lyme disease researchers apply similar electronic health records-based excess-incidence methods to estimate how much chronic Lyme disease is undercounted?

How was it checked? The numbers are copied from the two simulators. The wording is written by an A I model and checked in code, and any number the code cannot match is flagged. In this comparison, no numbers were flagged. The analysis used three model calls at a total cost of about twenty-eight cents. It is also placed on a chart among all twenty-five simulators, and a list shows which other simulators fill the gaps the pair leaves.

The limits: This analysis rests on a small, non-random set of papers: exactly one Lyme disease paper and one Long COVID paper, each built from a single uploaded document with its own self-selected or network-specific sample, so no conclusions about either condition in general can be drawn, and findings on dimensions not covered by either paper's own data cannot be filled in or inferred. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context and questions. It does not measure a difference between the diseases.

The potential: in principle, the same approach could be applied to any published paper focused on Lyme disease or Long COVID, which could be transformative. That is a potential, not yet shown beyond those two conditions. It is part of D B bun's infrastructure, and the concept could be extended to any disease, with a vision of wide use, for example by PubMed, the National Library of Medicine, and libraries. That is a vision, not something tested.
