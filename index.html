Frequently Asked Questions

What this page covers

A general reference for how DBbun works and how to read what it produces, independent of any one document. When you're reviewing a specific simulation, its own Operation Flow tab shows that simulation's real numbers at each step described below.

Which federal resources are the most valuable, and why?

This ranking is overall, across the whole library: the simulations in the library. Resources are ranked by what each one lets the system do for a simulator, not by size. Some apply to one condition only; the label under each bubble says which. The "informs N of 25" numbers are a snapshot taken on 2026-10-05, when the library had 25 simulations (12 Lyme disease, 13 Long COVID). "Shown" means offered to the AI model as background.

Two patterns stand out. Value is concentrated in a few small, checkable sources (CDC surveillance, the inflation index, the health-center visit survey, the Long COVID prevalence survey) rather than in the size of the corpus. And "shown" is not "used": only a computed cross-check proves a federal number was compared with the document, so the number on each bubble says how many of the 25 simulators the source was shown to, listed beside, or checked against. It is not a measure of how much the source shaped a result.

How does the simulator actually work?
  • You upload a document. DBbun extracts its text and, separately, a local machine-learning classifier decides whether it's about Lyme disease, Long COVID, or both - no manual tagging needed.
  • That classification drives a retrieval step over a federal knowledge library of about 1,454 units from 12 agencies (including CDC, CDC/NCHS, CMS, AHRQ, Census, BLS, BEA, HRSA, SSA and NOAA): the most relevant pre-processed federal evidence packages are selected and included alongside your document.
  • Four AI (Claude/Sonnet) calls then run in sequence, matching the checklist you see while a simulation is generating:
    1. Cost-of-illness analysis - reads your document(s) plus the retrieved federal evidence and extracts the scientific findings (a plain-language summary, which costs/burdens the document actually reports, and limitations like claims-undercounting or a funding gap).
    2. Interactive simulator & documentation - turns that analysis into the actual interactive model: the inputs, outputs, and formulas connecting them, plus the takeaway/next-step text for each audience and the written documentation.
    3. What-if scenarios & forecasts - using the already-built, already-validated inputs and outputs, generates the named one-click scenarios and the multi-period forecast trajectories on the Forecast tab.
    4. Downloadable Python source - generates a standalone Python version of the same model for the bundle; runs separately and does not block or change the interactive result above if it fails.
  • Nothing here queries a live database or a live internet search at generation time - every federal fact came from the pre-built local library, and every document-specific fact came from what you uploaded.
What does combining the paper, federal data and the simulator reveal?

Each piece alone leaves something hidden; together they show more. The diagram shows what each adds and what each overlap means. Hover any region for its meaning, or read the seven cases below, one per region, with a real example.

The paper alone: its own figures, in one price year and one place. The numbers exactly as the PDF prints them, for example a cost of $1,965 per patient in 2000 dollars, from one region. You cannot adjust them, compare them with today's prices, or see what they leave out.

Federal data alone: national context, not this paper in particular. Federal sources describe the country, for example about 35,000 Lyme cases reported to CDC in a typical year. They cannot tell you what a particular paper's patients cost, and some cannot identify Lyme at all (MEPS).

The simulator alone: what-ifs, forecasts and 16 burden types. The tool's machinery: adjustable assumptions, named scenarios, a forecast, and the 16 burden categories. On its own it is empty; it needs a paper's evidence to fill it.

Paper and federal data: checked and restated. The system takes figures the paper reports and compares them with federal data, in code. A dollar amount is restated in current dollars ($1,965 in 2000 is about $3,674 today, so the paper alone understates today's burden by 1.87 times). A case count is compared with CDC's (that paper's 23,763 national cases equal CDC's reported count).

Paper and simulator: burdens the paper never measures. Once a paper's results are sorted into the 16 burden categories, the empty categories show what the paper leaves out. Across the 25 simulations (12 Lyme disease, 13 Long COVID) the average paper measures 1.6 of the 16 and none measures more than 4, so most of the burden is invisible in any single paper.

Federal data and simulator: benchmarks beside your results. Each simulator's Evidence tab lists federal figures that fit it, for example the 15.9% of U.S. adults who cared for a friend or family member in the past 30 days. They let you read the paper's numbers against national context. They are context only: they do not feed the simulator's numbers.

All three: adjustable and checked, with the gaps stated. Adjustable means you can change the paper's assumptions in the number boxes and watch the results move. Checked means the paper's figures were compared with federal data by code, not by the AI model. Gaps stated means the caveats and the unmeasured burdens are shown, not hidden. Example: a paper's 35,000 reported Lyme cases matched CDC's 34,973 (within 0.1%), and its 476,000 claims-based estimate is 13.6 times the reported count; in the simulator you can change the assumptions behind that gap and see what moves.

These are examples from real runs, not a controlled study. Only some papers report figures that federal data can check (14 of the 25 simulators computed at least one federal check, and 9 of those compared a figure with federal data; the others only restated dollar amounts in current dollars), and the burden categories are assigned by an AI model, with at most one category per result.

What do "observed," "assumed," and "projected" mean?
  • Observed - a value the source document actually reported or measured.
  • Assumed - a value DBbun filled in because the document didn't report it, always clearly labeled as an assumption, never presented as if it were observed.
  • Projected - a value computed forward from an observed or assumed rate of change (used on the Forecast tab), a straight-line continuation, not a prediction that real-world change will stay steady.
  • This labeling is the single thing DBbun is most consistent about: every number you see traces back to one of these three, never an unexplained figure.
What do the burden categories mean?

When a result clearly represents one of these kinds of burden, DBbun optionally tags it so the Explore tab can group it into a named section. A document is never forced to touch every category - most won't, and that's expected.

These categories overlap as ideas, and that is expected. One real situation can touch several: a delayed diagnosis can cost a job and lead to uncovered spending. Each result (an output or a forecast) gets at most one tag, the best fit for the quantity it measures, so one simulator can have results in several categories.

Tags group results; they do not split a burden into shares that add up. Do not add numbers across categories or results unless the document itself does, because the same cost or hour can appear in more than one place (double counting). To choose between neighbors, ask what is being counted (money, time, harm, a score, or a gap) and who bears it.

How did real people shape this simulator?

DBbun was created by Dr. Uri Kartoun, founder of DBbun LLC. It was built by listening as well as reading: people who live with these illnesses, care for someone who does, or work on them were contacted, interviewed or asked questions, and the product changed because of what they said. Since August 2026, more than 100 people were contacted online and 8 in-depth interviews were held (Long COVID patients, caregivers and advocates, researchers, and two government officials). All 80 public stories and ideas shared in the LymeX Healthathon, a national Lyme storytelling challenge, were analyzed, and 17 questions about hidden costs were posted on its public discussion board; 15 received replies from firsthand Lyme patients (among them a nurse practitioner, an epidemiologist, a medical-anthropology researcher and a former federal working-group member). Below, each person or group points to what changed; open one to read what was heard. Everyone is described by role only; nobody is named, and nobody has endorsed the product.

Does the simulator recommend or evaluate treatments?

No. It turns one uploaded paper's own evidence into an interactive model of burden and cost: what an illness costs in money, time, work, schooling and care. It never recommends, ranks or scores treatments, protocols, supplements or providers on its own, and it does not tell anyone what to do about their own health.

"Simulator" here means a what-if model of cost and burden that you can adjust (for example, what if diagnosis took a year less), not a treatment simulator. If a paper reports what a treatment costs or what it did, the simulator shows that as the paper's own reported evidence, labeled observed, assumed or projected, never as advice. For care decisions, talk with a clinician.

Can I use the simulators from another system or script (API)?

Yes. The full tool, run on your own computer, has a small programming interface (API) so another system can call a finished simulator or submit papers without a browser. The read-only public demo cannot run it.

  • Run a finished simulator. Send your own assumptions and get every output and forecast back at once. It uses the same arithmetic as this page, costs nothing, and rejects values outside an input's own range or inputs the source paper fixes.
  • Submit papers and come back later. Papers join a first-in, first-out line and run one at a time (about 4 to 9 minutes and roughly $0.25 to $0.85 in model fees each). A script then collects the finished bundles.
  • Access. Every call needs a key made on your own machine, and by default the interface answers only on your own computer.

Steps and examples are in the API guide (the file API.md); the machine-readable description is at /api/v1/openapi.json. Results are illustrative simulations built from the source papers, not clinical advice or statistical forecasts.

What is a cross-pollination analysis?

It compares two simulators side by side: one built from a Lyme disease paper and one from a Long COVID paper. In the Simulation library, tick one of each and choose "Cross-pollination analysis". After about a minute it shows a table of what each simulator says about the same questions (cost, work, diagnostic delay, access to care, how official counts undercount), which of the 16 burdens each paper measures, and the key figures copied from each simulator. Once it exists, a link "Cross-pollination analysis available" appears under both papers, and choosing the same pair again opens the saved comparison.

The numbers are copied from the simulators. The wording is written by an AI model and checked in code against the simulators, and any number it cannot match is flagged. The two papers differ in population, year and method, so the comparison gives context; it does not measure a difference between the diseases. Comparing more than two papers at once is planned.

What role do federal data play in creating a simulator?

The simulator's numbers come from your uploaded document. Federal data plays four supporting roles:

  1. It decides what is eligible. The detected condition (Lyme disease or Long COVID) determines which federal datasets can be searched.
  2. It is offered as background. About 10 short excerpts from pre-processed federal summaries are shown to the model alongside your document. The model may cite one only if its population, definition, unit and timeframe genuinely fit.
  3. It is used in computed cross-checks. Every computed comparison ends with a provenance line: the kind of federal data (survey self-report, clinical visit record, surveillance report or price index), its period, and that code, not the AI model, did the arithmetic. Federal figures are published or surveyed values; the simulator's own outputs are modeled from your document. The model only reads certain quantities out of your document (dollar amounts with a price year, reported or estimated Lyme case counts, national Long COVID prevalence percentages). The system then compares them with federal data in code: it restates dollar figures in current dollars and compares case counts with CDC reported cases. A quantity it cannot check is shown as not computed, with the reason.
  4. It is shown as benchmarks. Every simulator's Evidence tab lists up to five federal benchmarks that fit its condition and burden categories, for example national Lyme case counts, Long COVID prevalence, or hours adults spend on health care paperwork. They are context for reading the results, not inputs: none of them fed the simulator's numbers. Each shows where it comes from, its period, and how reliable it is.

Limits of this approach (also stated in each simulator's Evidence tab, documentation PDF and Excel export):

  • Federal data does not supply this simulator's numbers. They come from your uploaded document; federal data selects which sources are eligible, is offered to the model as background, and is used in the computed cross-checks.
  • Retrieval is keyword-based and runs once per document, choosing about 10 excerpts rather than one per figure, so the best match for a specific figure can be missed.
  • Only three kinds of figures are cross-checked in code: dollar amounts (restated in current dollars with the CPI-U inflation index, 1913-2025 price years), Lyme reported or estimated case counts (compared with CDC surveillance), and national Long COVID prevalence percentages for 2022-2024 (compared with the CDC/Census Household Pulse Survey). Everything else is context only.
  • Coverage gaps: there is no NIH research-funding data yet, and Long COVID breakdowns by sex, age and race are not yet computed. MEPS releases a 3-character diagnosis code only when at least 30 sampled people carry it, so the Lyme code (A69) is missing from most recent years, and AHRQ advises against estimating Lyme costs from MEPS.
  • Federal data is a downloaded snapshot (mostly through 2023-2025) and is refreshed by hand.
  • Sources measure different things (surveillance reports, insurance claims, surveys) and change definitions over time, so a comparison shows whether numbers sit in the same range, not that either is right. Claims- and code-based sources also undercount.
How does DBbun relate to federal health-data standards (Blue Button 2.0, FHIR, TEFCA)?

DBbun is a local tool that turns an uploaded paper into a simulator. It does not connect to any health-record network, and it does not claim alignment with a standard it does not implement. Its downloads are PDF, Excel, CSV, JSON and Python source, not FHIR resources. Here is where it stands with each:

  • Blue Button 2.0 (CMS): a credible next step, planned and not built. It lets a Medicare beneficiary authorize an app to pull their own claims. In DBbun, a user could someday connect their own claims, with consent, as an input alongside an uploaded paper.
  • TEFCA and FHIR networks: designed for exchanging identified clinical records between care institutions. DBbun refuses uploads that contain Social Security numbers or medical-record-number and date-of-birth labels (it does not detect other kinds of identifiers), and it is not a care-coordination system, so it does not connect to them.
  • USCDI+: at the last check (August 2026) it had no cost-of-illness domain. A small, honest step would be tagging extracted diagnoses with their ICD-10-CM codes; that is not built.
  • CMS Interoperability Framework: DBbun echoes its consent and transparency criteria at its own scale. The extracted text, table captions and figures of your document reach the model (never the original file), no generated code executes, and every model call is recorded in the download. It claims no formal alignment.
What are the limitations ("Gaps and Boundaries")?

Four checks DBbun runs against every document, each only flagged when the document itself supports it - never inferred from general outside knowledge or from the condition's identity alone.

  • Claims/diagnosis-code undercounting - flagged when a cited figure is based on insurance claims or diagnosis codes, since real case-finding research shows these codes miss a large share of true cases - especially relevant for Lyme and Long COVID, which don't always have a clean diagnostic code.
  • Subgroup disaggregation - flagged when a document reports only one pooled, population-wide figure without checking whether it holds the same way across sex, age, race/ethnicity, disability status, and other groups - not a missing number, a missing check.
  • Funding gap - flagged when a document itself reports a real disparity in research funding or investment for this condition, compared to another condition or across time.
  • Systemic gap - flagged when a document describes a gap in the healthcare or research SYSTEM itself, not a cost to any one patient - for example, clinicians lacking training to recognize the condition, or findings with no clear path to becoming standard care.

What the simulators do not model.

  • Household burden. Each simulator describes one person or one cohort, as the uploaded paper does. Where several members of a household are affected, for example a parent and children, it does not add up the burden across them.
  • Care the paper does not price. Non-covered tests, supplements and other out-of-pocket care are counted only when the uploaded paper itself reports what they cost.
What is Cross-Pollination Analysis?
  • A separate mode, run from the Library, that synthesizes findings across multiple of your own past simulations at once - not within a single document, across several.
  • It identifies which cost-of-illness themes are shared between Lyme disease and Long COVID versus specific to one, turning isolated single-document extractions into a genuine cross-condition comparison.
  • You choose which past simulations to include - there's no implicit "use everything" default.
What happens to my uploaded document? Is my data private?
  • The extracted text, table captions and figures of your document are sent to the AI model. The original file itself is never uploaded to the model provider.
  • No generated code executes on your machine or DBbun's server - the downloadable Python source is for you to run yourself, not something DBbun runs on your behalf.
  • Every model call made for your simulation - what was sent, to which provider, and when - is recorded and disclosed, visible in the "Generation details and API usage" panel and the downloadable bundle's provenance file.
Can I trust the numbers?
  • Every number traces to either your uploaded document, a named federal source, or an explicitly labeled assumption - never an unexplained figure.
  • DBbun does not diagnose, recommend treatment, or replace your care team - it is a decision-support model for interpreting a document's own evidence.
  • Real defects found through live testing get fixed with a regression test, not just a one-off patch - the current automated test suite has 50+ tests and passes before every change ships.
Is the text written by AI, and is it watermarked?
  • Yes. The analysis, documentation, takeaways and downloadable Python source are written by an AI model (Claude Sonnet); the numbers you can change are computed by the simulator itself.
  • From September 30, 2026 the model provider applies an imperceptible statistical watermark to that model's text output, as required by the EU AI Act's transparency rules. It does not change the meaning, quality or numbers, and it contains no information about you or your document.
  • Simulators generated before that date are not watermarked. The bundle's NOTICE.txt carries the same statement.
What does the AI model already know on its own?
  • The model behind DBbun (Claude Sonnet) was trained on a large body of public text that probably includes many PubMed abstracts and open-access papers. What it was trained on is not published, paywalled full text is largely out of reach, and some publishers restrict the use of their content for AI training.
  • Every model also stops learning at a cutoff date. Recent papers, and Long COVID research in particular (a condition first described in 2020 and still moving fast), may be unknown to it.
  • For that reason DBbun never treats the model's background knowledge as evidence. A value should come from your uploaded document (with a page reference) or a cited federal source, and anything else is labeled an assumption. If you spot a figure that seems to come from nowhere, treat it as unverified and check it against the document.

Raw file, dataset, and review unit - what's the difference?
  • Raw file - a file exactly as downloaded from the federal agency (a spreadsheet, PDF, zip archive, etc.), stored on this computer under databases/raw/. The summary line at the top of this page shows how many there are.
  • Dataset - one named federal data program (for example, CDC's Lyme surveillance data, or AHRQ's MEPS survey). A dataset is usually made of several raw files (different years, states, or file parts). The table above groups the registered datasets by agency.
  • Review unit - one processed, evidence-ready knowledge package produced from a raw file (or from one part of it: a tab in a spreadsheet, one file inside a zip archive). It is a short derived text summary with the facts and caveats extracted from that file, not the raw file itself. Review units are what the retrieval step searches and what the AI model is shown excerpts of. A single raw file often splits into several review units, so the review-unit total is larger than the raw-file total.
What does "condition scope" mean?

Every review unit is tagged with which condition it's actually relevant to, based on its own content:

  • Lyme & Long COVID - directly relevant to both conditions.
  • Lyme only / Long COVID only - relevant to just one.
  • General / background - useful context (cost-of-living, wages, provider counts) that isn't specific to either condition.
  • Not yet classified - hasn't been re-tagged since the last content change; re-processing fixes this.
What happens when I click "Re-process selected"?
  1. The current review units for the selected dataset(s) are backed up to a timestamped folder under backups/ - nothing is deleted until a safe copy exists.
  2. Those review units are removed from the live index.
  3. The dataset's raw files are re-read from disk and turned back into review units, using that dataset's extractor (see below).
  4. Every review unit is re-tagged with its condition scope.
  5. If any step fails, the backup from step 1 is restored automatically - the live corpus is never left half-updated.

This is mostly deterministic parsing (reading spreadsheets, PDFs, and survey files) - no AI model call happens during re-processing.

What happens when I add a new resource?
  • It only registers the dataset's name, agency, and a note - it does not download or fetch any files. The raw files must already exist under databases/raw/<key>/ before you add it, or the form will reject it.
  • A newly added resource starts with 0 review units until you check its box and click "Re-process selected."
  • Until someone writes it a dedicated extractor, it uses the generic fallback reader (it detects whether the files are spreadsheets or text documents and processes them accordingly) - the same starting point every dataset here began from.
What is an "extractor"? Who wrote it?
  • An extractor is a piece of this project's own Python code that knows how to read one particular raw file format (a multi-tab Excel workbook, a fixed-width survey file, a zip of CSVs, etc.) and turn it into review units the simulator can cite as evidence.
  • Every extractor here was written for this project, by Claude (Anthropic's AI, working under Uri Kartoun's direction) as part of building this corpus - there is no separate vendor or team.
  • The "Extractor" column below names the actual source file and function (for example, scripts/build_first_100_knowledge.py:hcup()) - all under this project's own scripts/ folder on this computer.
  • It connects to the simulator indirectly: extractors only build the review-unit corpus ahead of time; when you generate a simulation, the simulator searches that already-built corpus for relevant review units, it never reads raw federal files directly.
Where does machine learning actually happen?
  • Not on this Administrator page. Everything here (re-processing, adding a resource) is deterministic file parsing - no AI model is called.
  • Machine learning runs later, on the Generate page, at the moment you upload a document: a classifier first determines whether it's about Lyme, Long COVID, or neither, then a retrieval model scores every review unit in this corpus for relevance to that document and selects the ones the AI model is shown as evidence.
  • It always searches whatever is currently in this corpus - it never changes the corpus itself, so re-processing here is what determines what later generations can find.
How was this list of resources chosen?
  • Most of this corpus (27 of the current families) was acquired and formally reviewed over Aug 14-16, 2026, before this app's Administrator page existed - each source was accepted for a specific, named modeling role (e.g. BEA regional economics for lost-income/productivity denominators, HRSA for provider-access and diagnostic-delay context), not just because a list named it. That acquisition was then explicitly locked: new sources are only added when one of five specific triggers is true (the simulator can't compute a required output, a field can't be safely interpreted, provenance is wrong, validation shows a real coverage/bias problem, or HHS/TOPx feedback explicitly requires it) - "cosmetic catalog improvements and speculative extra datasets do not reopen" that lock.
  • Not the same as the official TOPx/HealthData.gov challenge resource page. Every item on that organizer-provided list has been checked against this corpus: MEPS, HCUP, CMS/NHEA, ACS, SSA, CDC, and PubMed are already covered. The BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey's bulk microdata was attempted but BLS itself denied automated retrieval (a real, disclosed limitation, kept on record under bls/consumer_expenditure_2024) - a different BLS product, its own regularly published income-bracket tables, was acquired instead on 2026-09-21 (bls/consumer_expenditure_income_2012_2024), closing this gap for real household healthcare/insurance spending by income level. Physician Utilization & Payment, Geographic VA Expenditures, SSI Condition-Specific Statistics, and generic CDC chronic-disease-cost pages were deliberately evaluated and NOT added, for lacking a sufficiently direct Lyme or Long COVID signal. The "DOL Open Data Portal" item was previously logged as covered based on this project having BLS data - corrected 2026-09-21: BLS and the Department of Labor's own separate open data portal (data.dol.gov) are different things, and the actual DOL portal (42 real datasets checked directly) turned out to hold only mine-safety, workplace-injury, and unemployment-claims data - nothing with a direct Lyme or Long COVID cost signal, so nothing was added from it. BEA and HRSA, conversely, were added even though the organizer's page never named them, because they filled a real modeling role the page's list didn't cover.
  • That means the number of datasets shown for an agency reflects a real, reviewed decision for that agency - not the true size of its full public catalog, and not a one-to-one mirror of the challenge's own resource page either.
  • This list can still grow when the lock's own conditions are met (see "What happens when I add a new resource?" above) - it's a living collection with a real change-control rule behind it, not an unreviewed one.
Are all registered datasets actually used?
  • No, not all of them. A review unit is only ever shown to the AI model if it's tagged Lyme- or Long-COVID-specific, or its dataset is one of a short list explicitly allowed in even though it's general-purpose (currently AHRQ MEPS, CMS NHEA, BLS CPI and BLS OEWS), or - for BEA regional economics only - a document names a matching U.S. state.
  • Every other general-purpose dataset is fully processed and stored, but none of its review units are ever retrieved for a simulator. Rows in the table where this applies are marked "Never shown to the model."
  • This is a separate question from whether a dataset was worth ACQUIRING (see "How was this list of resources chosen?" above - that was carefully reviewed for every dataset here). Whether its general-purpose content should be RETRIEVABLE is a narrower, later, more technical question. A few exclusions were deliberately tested for this specifically (BEA's 810 units, kept out to avoid crowding out condition-specific evidence). Most of the rest were simply never evaluated for the retrieval list either way, and some may be worth adding to it later.

    • A web search returns a blended, unsourced answer from many pages; every number here traces to one page, table, or section of this specific document.
    • Reading the source yourself gets the same numbers, not a live model - here, changing one assumption recomputes every downstream figure correctly.
    • This does not replace your care team: it is a decision-support model for interpreting this document's own evidence, not a diagnosis or an individual treatment plan.
